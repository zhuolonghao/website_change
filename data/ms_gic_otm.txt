INVESTMENT STRATEGY
As October begins, investors should count themselves lucky. Despite
a surge in interest rates, ongoing geopolitical tension, higher oil and
diesel fuel prices, and reassessment of Federal Reserve policy, US
equities have been resilient. The S&P 500, up 12% for the year to
date, sits just 1.9% below its all-time high, while exhibiting relatively
low volatility at the cap-weighted index level. The conventional
60/40 stock/bond portfolio, meanwhile, has delivered a 5.4% year-to-
date return. That’s not a result one might expect near the end of a six-
month period when the two- and 10-year US Treasury yields,
respectively, increased roughly 150 and 110 basis points, with the
benchmark 10-year note at one point exceeding 5.3%, a 24-year high.
Equity market resilience reﬂects accelerating economic growth, stable labor
markets, improving earnings expectations and an AI-capex boom apparently
insensitive to borrowing costs and oil prices. Perhaps even more constructively,
valuations have declined from 22.5 times forward earnings in January to about 19.5
—in line with a rising cost of capital, even amid proﬁt-growth forecasts of 30% for
2026 and 17% for 2027. Several economic indicators have reinforced the growth
narrative recently. Namely, purchasing managers’ indexes are at ﬁve-year highs,
capital-goods orders are expanding close to 15% on an annualized basis and
personal consumption is up 3.8% annualized, while nominal second quarter GDP
growth is estimated to have reached 8.3%. Equally important, despite Fed
handwringing and related rhetoric, while inﬂation is close to 3%, it has not been
accelerating, and market-based expectations remain anchored near 2.5%.
That is not to say that rising rates don’t matter. We acknowledge that damage has
been done below the market’s surface and beyond the strength of the AI trade.
Small-cap US equities are off approximately 10% since Aug. 14, as the equal-
weighted index has fallen around 6.5%. Market breadth has deteriorated sharply. At
the end of September, only 20.6% of stocks were above their 50-day moving
average—down from nearly 70% in the summer—while the median stock had
declined more than 15% from its 52-week high. Rate-sensitive regional bank stocks,
consumer durables, cyclicals and housing-linked equities have suffered, as markets
have reconcentrated around the AI ecosystem. More importantly, history suggests
that the speed and magnitude of the rate repricing will ultimately ﬁnd some victims.

Three Central Banks, One Direction
Seth B. Carpenter, Chief Global Economist, Morgan Stanley & Co. LLC
Resilient growth and renewed energy pressures are pushing
the Fed, ECB and BOJ toward higher rates. The paths differ,
but the implication is similar—more tightening than markets
expected earlier this year, alongside a ﬁrmer US dollar.
Over the past two weeks, our economists have revised their
outlooks for the Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank
and the Bank of Japan to include more rate hikes. Each
economy faces different challenges, but all three central
banks are arriving at a similar conclusion: Growth has
remained remarkably resilient despite large shocks, and
renewed energy price pressures have increased the risk that
inﬂation will prove more persistent than previously expected.
The clearest example, and our biggest revision, is the Fed.
While for much of the year we had thought the Fed would
avoid hiking rates, now in addition to the increase at the
September FOMC meeting, we expect two more hikes, in
December and March, bringing the terminal rate to 4.25%–
4.50%. While Chair Warsh highlighted the inﬂationary
implications of higher energy and commodity prices, the more
important signal was the assessment that policy is still not
sufﬁciently restrictive. Although Chair Warsh said that he
does not think that a neutral-rate estimate is helpful in
making policy decisions, he nevertheless described the
September move as “removing a dose of accommodation,” 
and the rest of the FOMC did revise its estimate of the
neutral policy rate upward (albeit very slightly).
In our view, the Fed appears to be reassessing not just the
outlook for inﬂation but the amount of restraint required to
bring it sustainably back to target. Still, we see the shift as
more of a recalibration than a fundamental change in policy,
so the market may have overestimated how much hiking is
left.
The shift has important market implications. Our rates
strategists expect investors to pull forward additional
tightening expectations in the near term while increasingly
questioning how long policy can remain at restrictive levels
before growth eventually slows. More broadly, a Fed that
appears more sensitive to energy-driven inﬂation pressures
strengthens the case for a ﬁrmer dollar. Over recent months,
rising energy prices supported the euro because investors
assumed the ECB would respond more aggressively than the
Fed. That perceived asymmetry may now be changing. If
investors conclude that both central banks are prepared to
lean against energy-related inﬂation risks, higher energy
prices become less euro-supportive and increasingly dollar-
supportive. Our foreign exchange strategists therefore
continue to favor dollar strength, particularly against the
Japanese yen.

Europe faces a similar inﬂation challenge, though through a
different transmission channel. The renewed rise in natural
gas and energy prices has led our economists to revise their
inﬂation outlook materially higher and to add another ECB
rate hike in December, followed by a much shallower easing
cycle in 2027. Importantly, however, energy prices alone do
not explain the change in outlook. Euro area activity has
proved more resilient than anticipated, reducing concerns
that modest additional tightening would derail growth. As a
result, the ECB is increasingly focused on preventing higher
energy costs from feeding into broader inﬂation dynamics,
even as policy rates move further into restrictive territory
(see chart).
Japan may appear different, but the underlying story is
surprisingly similar. For decades, the BOJ’s challenge was
generating inﬂation. Today, policymakers are increasingly
concerned about the possibility that inﬂation could overshoot
target. After the BOJ’s hike last week, we expect it to raise
rates to 1.5% in December, and then to 1.75% In March.

Like the Fed and ECB, the BOJ faces an economy that has

resilience in growth has changed the policy conversation.
Policymakers increasingly appear to believe that economies
can withstand higher rates than previously assumed and that
inﬂation risks remain uncomfortably elevated. For investors,
the next year is increasingly likely to be characterized by
higher policy rates and a stronger dollar than markets

GLOBAL POLICY
AI Sovereignty: Policy, Market and
Economic Implications
Ariana Salvatore, Equity Strategist, Morgan Stanley & Co. LLC
Daniel K. Blake, Equity Strategist, Morgan Stanley Asia (Singapore) Pte.+
Stephen C. Byrd, Equity Analyst, Morgan Stanley & Co. LLC
Sovereign control over critical AI infrastructure, models and
supply chains is a strategic imperative for governments. This
should increase the AI capex opportunity—$860 billion in the
US this year alone—and the risk of broader technology
restrictions. We see the resulting fragmentation of spending
across US, Chinese and regional AI ecosystems creating
opportunities in several sectors.
“Sovereign AI” is becoming a key principle for technology,
industrial and national-security policy. We see it as a country’s
ability to develop and deploy AI with meaningful control over
the underlying infrastructure, data, talent, energy, supply
chains and technology stack. That does not imply seeking full
self-sufﬁciency: We believe it’s more plausible to expect
governments to combine domestic capacity with selective
reliance on trusted partners, while reducing exposure to
adversarial technology providers, legal regimes and
infrastructure.
This national-security framing is central to our still-
constructive view on AI-capex spending in spite of political
pushback in the US (see chart). Given the geopolitical realities
(see our July 14 report, “Power Struggle: AI, Data Centers &
Local Backlash”), we do not expect the US government to
impose a broad pause or nationwide moratorium on data-
center development. 
We think the progression from AI competition to the physical
layer of the data-center build-out means that federal
lawmakers recognize the strategic imperative of AI and are
likely to continue supportive policy—from permitting reform
to Defense Production Act expansion—facilitating, rather
than reversing, the build-out. Said differently, compute
capacity is increasingly viewed as strategic infrastructure, and
restricting domestic build-out would risk weakening US
competitiveness relative to China.
This competitive backdrop underscores our view for AI policy
after the US midterm elections: AI policy initiatives seem
likely to take shape regardless of the election outcome,
reﬂecting bipartisan alignment around AI as a strategic
priority, but with emerging regulatory and societal
constraints.
This shift toward AI as a strategic, capital-intensive “arms
race,” with policy as an enabler, should continue to support
the AI investment cycle and associated infrastructure
demand, albeit with a slightly different approach depending
on the composition of Congress.

We see election outcomes as more likely to shape the pace

Regulation, meanwhile, is likely to remain incremental and
fragmented: Core areas like AI safety, data governance and

THE DE-RISKING POLICY TOOLKIT. A “de-risking” pattern
between the US and China has evolved as both sides pursue
national security interests over economic efﬁciencies. That’s
come alongside a more active government role in strategically
relevant sectors; governments are using either policy tools—
including federal incentives such as grants, loans and CHIPS
Act-style incentives—or more blunt instruments to insulate
key supply chains, like tariffs, under a variety of authorities.
Sovereign AI ﬁts within that broader shift from economic
integration toward technological multipolarity. The US and
China are increasingly prioritizing national security and
resilience over economic efﬁciency, even where this results in
higher costs, duplicated capacity or reduced market access.
In that vein, we see an increasing probability of both US and
Chinese AI policy intervention, which could lead to a greater
bifurcation of the global market. The de-risking is most stark

in the higher-tech segments of the economy, where

In our view, this backdrop—and the geopolitical realities that

key reason that the US is unlikely to enact a nationwide data-

CHINA’S EXPANDING MARKET. Given the increasing
competition between the US and China, we see a larger
global total addressable market for Chinese AI-solution
providers than the market appreciates.
China’s AI is entering a new phase—one deﬁned less by
catching up in capability and more by capturing value. The
country’s AI narrative has shifted decisively from training to
inference, from technology to application and from potential
to real earnings. Rather than competing purely at the frontier,
China is optimizing for speed, cost efﬁciency and system-level
integration, enabling AI to diffuse rapidly across the real
economy (see our May 10 report, “China AI 2.0.”).
Hence, China may not need to lead at the technological
frontier to build a commercially signiﬁcant AI ecosystem.
Focusing on inference, deployment speed, cost efﬁciency and
system integration may support faster adoption across both
industrial and consumer applications.
BEYOND CHIP CONTROLS. A shift from physical products
with military end-uses that are the typical targets of export
controls to the digital realm creates a more complex
enforcement challenge; AI models can be downloaded,
accessed through application programming interfaces, hosted
in third countries or deployed through offshore cloud
infrastructure. That raises the question: What tools can
governments use to implement and enforce export controls
on things like model usage? 
The distinction between physical and digital restrictions is
important. Chip and equipment controls constrain the ability
to develop future systems. Model, cloud and distribution
controls limit access to capabilities that already exist. In our
view, policymakers will continue to prioritize both.
We therefore see chips and semiconductor-manufacturing
equipment as likely to remain critical, but governments are
likely to place greater emphasis on the infrastructure through
which AI is trained, hosted, distributed and accessed.
Potential policy tools include the following:
Restrictions on offshore data-center and cloud capacity
Know-your-customer and end-user veriﬁcation
requirements
Reporting obligations for advanced compute
Limits on hosting or distributing designated foreign models
Model-marketplace and app-store restrictions
Procurement and cybersecurity standards
What do we think is likely? We see a more restrictive
environment overall ahead, given public statements from the
administration and the inherent national security concerns
highlighted by various groups of stakeholders, including
industry and government ofﬁcials. 
In practice, that likely means a middle path that seeks to
balance two objectives: ﬁrst, permitting a certain amount of
open-weight models to facilitate proliferation, model
development and access; and second, preventing theft of
American intellectual property in frontier large language
models (LLMs). In our view, that could be achieved by putting
certain Chinese AI model developers on a restricted list and
prohibiting usage of those companies’ models by US ﬁrms.
AI SOVEREIGNTY’S INVESTMENT IMPLICATIONS. In our
thematic investing, we now include “AI Sovereignty & Semi
Localization” as a subtheme that identiﬁes and quantiﬁes
exposure to the trend across more than 3,800 companies
globally. For almost a decade, we have had a research focus
on China’s semiconductor localization trend, and this remains
a core part of the AI competition dynamic, given the efforts to
reduce dependency on US technology. We have now
broadened the scope to include broader beneﬁciaries from
the sovereign AI theme.
From a sector perspective, this brings in a raft of global
companies that have exposure to compute infrastructure and
the AI model and application layer, including trusted vendors
in infrastructure software, cybersecurity and information
technology services. National champion telecom companies
and conglomerates are committing investments to position as
preferred partners for clients in the government and critical
infrastructure sectors. We also see capital goods and
materials companies as beneﬁciaries of the investment
supercycle, including those related to the additional capex
needed to improve AI sovereignty (see chart).

Our stronger-earnings-plus-lower-multiples narrative for
2026 tells the story, but the divergence between the S&P
500 Index and market breadth must be reconciled. If bond
volatility doesn’t ease, we lean toward a meeting in the
middle. Stick with large-cap quality. 
While the S&P 500 has made little headway since early June,
adjustments beneath the index surface have been signiﬁcant.
As we have emphasized all year, earnings strength is doing
the heavy lifting as higher energy prices and interest rates
pressure valuations. That also makes higher crude prices—
and especially those for reﬁned products—more of an
inﬂation risk than a growth risk given the strength of the
business/earnings cycle (see chart).

Earnings-per-share (EPS) revisions breadth continues to
improve, led by the higher-quality S&P 500 versus the lower-
quality Russell 2000. In fact, recent S&P 500 EPS revisions
strength points to a performance catch-up. This is classic
midcycle behavior, in our view. It also lines up nicely with our
hotter-but-shorter cycle framework, which we introduced
during COVID.
MIDCYCLE DYNAMICS. We continue to disagree that an S&P
500 just below an all-time high overlooks the numerous risks
facing equity markets. More than 50% of Russell 3000 Index
stocks have declined at least 20% since June, while the S&P
500 forward price/earnings (P/E) ratio is back near 19, which
was the March low. Meanwhile, median-stock EPS growth is
in the mid-teens, and index earnings revisions breadth is
approaching cycle highs. In other words, earnings have
absorbed signiﬁcant valuation compression at the index level
—a classic midcycle dynamic.
Many of the groups experiencing the largest drawdowns are
typical early-cycle winners that in many cases meaningfully
outperformed from the trough of the April 2025–June 2026
rolling recession. In June, we made the call that earnings
revisions breadth was peaking from a rate-of-change
standpoint for early-cycle winners, a key signpost indicating
an upcoming transition from early cycle to midcycle and
quality factor leadership. The Federal Reserve’s more hawkish
pivot is another sign that we have moved from early cycle to
midcycle.
Diving further into the breadth situation, the share of S&P
500 stocks above their 200-day moving average rose from
59% in late May to roughly 75% over the summer before
falling to 49% recently. Importantly, breadth was still
expanding as crude oil and yields moved higher. The sharper
deterioration came after the Fed’s Jackson Hole meeting. We
believe that suggests the recent narrowing is more about the
additional tightening being discounted. The same shift is
visible in rates. Faster nominal GDP growth and higher energy
prices drove much of the ﬁrst-half rise in yields, but since late
August, the move has increasingly reﬂected the Fed’s hawkish
pivot, which is happening against a strong growth backdrop—
i.e., not bad for equities overall even if it does impact
leadership. Nevertheless, we believe this divergence between
breadth and the S&P 500 needs to reconcile before the bull
market fully resumes.
CONTEMPLATING THE FED. Chair Warsh’s message at
Jackson Hole was not simply that inﬂation needed to fall, but
that it needed to fall “fast enough,” suggesting that ﬁnancial
conditions were not restrictive enough for the new chair.
September’s hike reinforced that reaction function. Still,
greater willingness to tighten does not tell us how much
tightening is ultimately required. At approximately 4.8%, the
two-year US Treasury yield is up since Jackson Hole, as the
bond market has potentially moved to an overly hawkish
near-term posture. The good news is that the Treasury term
premium remains well behaved, which throws some cold
water on fears around ﬁscal sustainability, or a Fed that is far
behind the curve.
On the other side of the equation, Chair Warsh is a new
leader who may not share the philosophy of the four prior
Fed chairs or the members of the FOMC today. Most notably,
Warsh is much further in the monetarist camp and believes
that inﬂation is determined more by money supply growth.
That creates additional uncertainty for investors to
contemplate what a Warsh-era Fed could mean for ﬁnancial
markets. More speciﬁcally, while the market may be assuming
too many rate hikes over the next year, it may be misjudging
Warsh’s willingness to use the balance sheet to either fund
ﬁscal deﬁcits or stabilize ﬁnancial conditions at the ﬁrst sign

CHINA
China’s Next Industrial Revolution
Sheng Zhong, Equity Analyst, Morgan Stanley Asia Limited+
Robin Xing, Chief China Economist, Morgan Stanley Asia Limited+
Chetan Ahya, Chief Asia Economist , Morgan Stanley Asia Limited+
China is entering a new phase of industrial development,
moving beyond its historical advantages of low-cost and
manufacturing scale toward ecosystem and innovation
leadership. We see profound implications for its industrial
supercycle, manufacturing proﬁt migration and position in
global manufacturing value chains.
Every major industrial revolution has reshaped the global
economic order. Great Britain led mechanization through
steam power in the 19th century. The US dominated mass
production and electriﬁcation in the 20th century. Germany
and Japan became synonymous with industrial precision and
engineering excellence. And the digital revolution of the past
three decades has elevated the US into the world’s dominant
software and internet power.
With the rise of AI, we believe the next industrial revolution is
around the corner and will become the most consequential
transition for industry since the rise of electriﬁcation and the
internet. Unlike previous industrial revolutions centered
primarily on technological progress, the next one—which we
call China’s Industry 5.0—is simultaneously a productivity
revolution, geopolitical response, manufacturing
reorganization and strategic capital supercycle.
Since China began to open up in the late 1970s, its industrial
base has undergone a continual upgrade and today accounts
for around 28% of the value-add in global manufacturing. The
country’s manufacturing has evolved into a large, cost-
competitive and efﬁcient base.
We believe the country’s scale and global leading industries,
such as solar power, batteries, electric vehicles (EVs) and
drones, will extend into more frontier industries, such as
embodied AI—essentially physical AI that interacts with and
learns from its environment. The new industrial revolution in
China follows the ages of mechanization, electriﬁcation,
automation and digitalization (see chart). 
INDUSTRIAL LANDSCAPE TRANSFORMED. In the next
decade, we expect China’s manufacturing leadership to evolve
from scale and cost toward ecosystem and industrial
innovation leadership. We see ﬁve transformational trends to
come.
First, AI will be at the heart of manufacturing operating
systems. The largest productivity gains from steam and
electricity came only after factories reorganized around them,
and we believe AI will follow the same pattern: Factory and
operational platforms will be built with AI as their core
architecture, actively coordinating workﬂows, predicting
problems and making decisions rather than just acting as an
added software tool. China has the world’s largest physical
manufacturing base, and more than 100 million industrial
devices are already digitally connected. The strategic
opportunity is to turn those connected assets into common
sensing, reasoning and execution systems.
Second, physical labor will become “software-upgradable”
capital equipment. Manufacturing will be increasingly
conducted by groups of embodied AI, including various robots
—humanoid, industrial and collaborative—as well as
autonomous mobile robots, autonomous guided vehicles and
vertical take-off and landing vehicles (VTols). Software
upgrades will improve capital equipment, extending
automation from highly standardized production into ﬂexible
high-mix manufacturing and converting deployment into data
generation.

WHY CHINA IS SET TO LEAD. China is not building Industry
5.0 from scratch: It is based on an industrial system already
operating at unmatched scale and full product category
coverage. 
In this system, Chinese government policy generally
establishes long-term direction, lowers early adoption
barriers and often acts as both a growth accelerator and a risk
mitigator. Meanwhile, large downstream customers provide
qualiﬁcation and operating data, while local industry clusters
connect equipment, components, materials and software, as
engineers and competing ﬁrms rapidly reﬁne products.
China’s ﬁnancial system reinforces this process, providing
relatively stable, long-tenor funding for strategic sectors,
while allowing companies to absorb lengthy qualiﬁcation
cycles before commercial returns emerge. Well-developed
and highly efﬁcient infrastructure enables fast, low-cost
product iteration and accelerates deployment.
China’s rise in new energy vehicles (NEV) provides the
clearest example. China reached approximately 51% of global
NEV sales in 2025 after combining central policy direction,
infrastructure support, local-government competition,
complete industrial clusters and intense private-sector
iteration. China’s battery, motor, power-electronics and
charging ecosystems then created a reinforcing scale
advantage.
DECADE-LONG CAPEX SUPERCYCLE. Industrial revolutions
have always been capex-intensive. We expect around Rmb340
trillion ($50 trillion) in cumulative industrial investment in
2026–2035, with Industry 5.0 creating about Rmb80 trillion
($12 trillion) of incremental capex demand. 
As AI diffusion raises demand for both intangible and physical
investment, the global capex supercycle should support
external demand and help absorb overcapacity. We thus
expect the industrial capex/GDP ratio to rise from 17.4% in
2025 to roughly 19.5% by 2035, which is slightly higher than
its previous peak of 19.2% in 2011, providing a counterweight
to structurally weaker property and traditional infrastructure
capex.
Based on our estimates, external funding already accounted

2020–2025. We expect bank loans to remain the anchor at
around 47% of funding over the next decade. Corporate
retained earnings and owned capital should provide roughly
one-quarter to one-third of funds. Fiscal funds and capital
markets in China and Hong Kong should provide the strategic
and risk-bearing layers (see chart).

GLOBAL IMPLICATIONS. China is not the only beneﬁciary of

goods demand, with different regions capturing different

NOT A LINEAR MOVE. Industrial revolutions have not always
progressed smoothly. Railroads, telecom and semiconductor
companies, solar power and EVs all experienced prolonged

may initially suppress returns before creating long-term
winners.
The largest risk is not technological failure, but economically
inefﬁcient success. China excels at scaling capacity, cutting
costs and diffusing technology fast—but the same strengths
could breed overcapacity, deﬂation and margin compression if
demand cannot keep pace with supply. The road from
investment to productivity, proﬁts and income is thus likely to

pace and quality.

Millennials Are Wealthier Than You
Think
Sarah Wolfe, Investment Strategist, Morgan Stanley Wealth Management
The millennial generation (Gen Y) was supposed to be the
one that fell behind. But its members now hold more than
twice the inﬂation-adjusted wealth per person than members
of Gen X did at the same age. Where it came from and who
inside the cohort actually owns it offer important lessons for
ﬁnancial planning.  
Ask most people to rank Gen X and Gen Y by ﬁnancial
standing at the same stage of life, and the majority would say
Gen X comes out ahead. Millions of millennials graduated
from college amid one of the worst labor markets in 80
years, and many of them have felt that the American dream
of making it to the middle class is out of reach.
But that’s no longer what the data shows.
Using the Federal Reserve’s distributed ﬁnancial accounts, we
compared Gen X (born between 1965 and 1980) and
millennials (born between 1981 and 1996) at the identical age.
The two cohorts are 16 years apart, which led us to compare
them on the following basis: Gen X in 2009, when the
generation spanned the ages of 29 to 44, against millennials
in 2025, in the same range of 29 to 44. In constant 1982–1984
dollars, millennials today hold $75,716 in net worth per
person, on average. Gen X, at the same age, held $32,009.
Millennials are ahead by 2.4 times (see chart).

A LEAD THAT DIDN’T LAST. When both generations were
young, Gen X was comfortably ahead. Through their twenties,
its members held a major advantage over where millennials
would stand at the same age—more than double at the
widest.
That lead narrowed steadily as members of both cohorts
moved through their careers, disappearing by 2020. Gen X
has not led at a matched age since. In fact, millennials have
held the advantage in every comparison-year since, and the
size of that advantage has only grown. The reversal traces to
a speciﬁc event: the COVID-era boom in stocks and housing. It
can also be explained by three key developments, roughly in
the following order.
AUTOMATION WINDOW. First, retirement savings got
automated after Gen X had already missed the window. The
Pension Protection Act of 2006 gave employers a clear, tax-
incentivized path to automatically enroll new hires in a 401(k)
rather than requiring them to opt in, typically defaulting them
into a target-date fund invested mostly in equities. According
to research from Vanguard (“How America Saves,” 25th
Edition, 2026), participation runs above 90% under auto-
enrollment versus roughly 60% when employees have to opt
in. One Government Accountability Ofﬁce comparison of
workers hired before and after their employer adopted auto-
enrollment (“Retirement System: A Comprehensive Re-
evaluation Is Needed to Better Promote Future Retirement
Security,” published in October 2017) found that participation
jumped from 37% to 86%. Gen X built its retirement savings
mostly on the older, opt-in system. Millennials came in on the
other side of that law, and the related impact started showing
up in the data years before the overall wealth gap reversed.
Real estate, another source of wealth, took longer to turn. In
2018, Gen X still held more than double millennials’ real
estate wealth per capita. That gap, which did not narrow
gradually, closed as millennials aged into their thirties and
started buying homes at the highest rate of any age group.
Millennials now enjoy a $13,632 per-capita real estate
advantage over Gen X on an age-matched basis (see following
chart).
AVOIDING THE MARKDOWN. Second, missing 2008 as
investors didn’t mean millennials escaped the Great Financial
Crisis (GFC)—it just hit them somewhere else. Born between
1965 and 1980, members of Gen X were between 28 and 43
when the crisis hit, old enough to own homes and hold equity
portfolios large enough to take a real hit when ﬁnancial
markets fell by half. Millennials, born between 1981 and 1996,
were between 12 and 27, mostly still in school or just starting
careers, with too little invested to have much exposure to the
crash. Gen X owned assets that were marked down in 2008
and 2009. Millennials, as a cohort, did not.
Notably, millennials graduating into that labor market paid a
real cost of their own—in earnings rather than assets.

ASSET-PRICE BOOM. Third, those setups subsequently
collided with the biggest asset-price boom of either
generation’s adult life. By 2020, many millennials had spent
more than a decade automatically enrolled and invested in
401(k)s, with few meaningful crisis-related losses from which
to recover. Gen Xers, by contrast, had already lived through
one crash and were investing on a slower, opt-in track
established a generation earlier. When stocks and home
prices surged during and after the pandemic, millennials had
more money already positioned to beneﬁt, and more time left
for it to compound. At that moment, the ranking ﬂipped, and
it has not ﬂipped back.
WHAT THE AVERAGE HIDES. Everything above describes the
aggregate. The aggregate, however, is not synonymous with
the individual household. Distributed Financial Accounts of
the United States data reports cohort wealth that we divide
by cohort population: a mean, not a median. That distinction
matters more here than almost anywhere else in economic
data because the largest driver of the millennial advantage is
also the most unevenly distributed major asset class.
Corporate equities and mutual funds now make up 17.5% of
the average millennial’s total assets, versus 4.9% for Gen X at
the same age; in dollar terms, millennial equity holdings are
5.7 times greater than those of Gen X. Equity ownership in
the US is concentrated among a relatively small share of
households. “Millennials hold 2.4 times the wealth Gen X did”
is a true statement about millennials overall; it is not
necessarily true about the millennial in the middle of the
group.
THE NEXT GENERATION. Gen Z is currently standing where
millennials once did: entering, or in, early adulthood during a
rapidly changing job market while being told a version of the
same story millennials heard. This time, it is a mix of how
artiﬁcial intelligence will create a more challenging labor
market for young adults, that housing affordability is at an all-
time low and that the massive run-up in equities is behind us.
There is reason to take that seriously. The bar for ﬁnancial
security has moved higher for every successive generation:

THIRD LEVER: LOWER BUYBACKS AND EQUITIES. So far,
hyperscalers have relied more on leases and debt than on
equity to fund their capex ambitions, although equity could
become more useful if interest rates rise, credit spreads

Rather than issuing shares, the hyperscalers have lately been
preserving cash by reducing buybacks (except for Microsoft,
which has yet to cut its share buybacks or issue debt).
Historically, a signiﬁcant portion of share repurchases was
intended to offset the dilution from companies’ stock-based
compensation (SBC), but that offset is waning.
For instance, Google has cut its share buybacks from more
than $60 billion a year to zero; it also issued $50 billion of
equity in the second quarter this year, resulting in more than

shareholders have begun to experience dilution as a result,
which is a big change: The company had reduced its share
count by 13% from its peak over the last decade.
Dilution overall could increase as total stock-based
compensation (SBC) continues to grow per unit of headcount
(see our June 8 report, “The State of Stock-Based
Compensation”). While this expense does not affect cash
ﬂow, it does affect investor economics. Given the need for
cash to build, we expect SBC-related dilution to increase.

involved in the AI build-out are ﬁnding new sources of
funding as well. Oracle, for example, recently began using

disclosing $4.6 billion in the most recent quarter. These
prepayments are considered deferred revenue on the balance

prepayment is received more than a year in advance of when
the revenue will be recognized.

Beyond the Oil Shock: What’s Next
for Energy Markets
On-again, off-again negotiations to end the conﬂict between
the US and Iran have led to greater volatility in the oil
markets over the past few months. And that may well
continue until the Strait of Hormuz ofﬁcially reopens,
according to Devin McDermott, equity and commodity
strategist at Morgan Stanley & Co. Research. While these ups
and downs may dominate headlines, they are hardly the only
story in the energy sector. McDermott recently spoke with
Morgan Stanley Wealth Management global strategist
Alfredo Pinel about the wider outlook for energy—from oil
and diesel to natural gas and electricity. Below is an edited
excerpt from their conversation on Sept. 25.
Alfredo Pinel (AP): To start with the question on many
investors’ minds, what is the outlook for crude oil prices,
given the uncertainty over the Iran war?
Devin McDermott (DM): Oil prices have generally been on the
rise again lately as some of the supply “buffers” diminish,
including strategic petroleum reserves. We’re calling for Brent
crude oil to trade around $100 per barrel by the fourth
quarter of 2026, and we’re not far from there right now. It is
certainly possible, if not likely, that we’ll continue to have
short-term volatility around that price point based on
escalation or de-escalation in the Middle East.
Once things reopen and normalize, we expect a gradual
decline in the price of crude oil. Assuming a restart of ﬂows
through the Strait of Hormuz in the fourth quarter, we would
expect the price of Brent to drop back down to the $70s by
later in 2027. So, while there has been a massive disruption,
it’s not a structural change, in our view.  It will eventually
normalize over several quarters.
AP: Switching to reﬁned products, the retail price for diesel in
the US has set record highs lately above $6.50 per gallon.
What’s driving that surge, and where is the impact felt most?
DM: Diesel is where we’re seeing the most acute tightness
right now. Currently, on a per-barrel basis, diesel costs over
$100 more than crude oil. That is four times the median
difference between the two over the past several decades.
We see two key drivers of diesel prices. First, about a million-
and-a-half barrels per day of reﬁning capacity have been
choked off from the global market behind the Strait of
Hormuz, some of that due to the closure of the strait and
some due to damage in the conﬂict. About one-third of the
lost reﬁning capacity—roughly half a million barrels per day
—is diesel.
The second driver is the ongoing conﬂict between Russia and
Ukraine. Drone strikes on Russian reﬁneries have increased
over the past several months, and as a result, a little over
three million barrels per day of reﬁning capacity is ofﬂine in
Russia now.
The combined lost supply from Russia and the Middle East,
by our estimate, is around 1.3 million barrels per day, or 15%
of the seaborne market. That’s very material and has put
signiﬁcant upward pressure on diesel prices, not just in the
US but around the world.
Diesel is generally not a direct consumer or retail-facing
product. It’s more business-to-business. It impacts the cost of
transit and supply chains across many industrial end-markets
and tends to show up on a lagged basis in consumer pricing
through various products, including food and household
goods.
AP: A group of US lawmakers has proposed a ban on diesel
exports. Do you think that will happen?
DM: The US exports a little over a million barrels per day of
diesel. Under a ban, those barrels would stay at home, but
there’s not enough domestic demand to consume all of it. 
Storage would be needed—but compared with oil, reﬁned
products tend to have much less storage capacity. Once
storage is ﬁlled, reﬁners would have to begin cutting runs, or
production—which means reducing not just diesel output but
also gasoline, jet fuel and heating oil, potentially driving those
prices higher. 
So, our view is that an outright ban on diesel exports is
unlikely, but more moderate adjustments and voluntary
restrictions are possible.
AP: War and geopolitics are affecting trade ﬂows in natural
gas as well. What’s your outlook for natural gas globally?
DM: The share of liqueﬁed natural gas that typically ﬂows
through the Strait of Hormuz is the same as that of oil: about
20% of the world’s supply. Historically, most of that gas went
to Asia, with a portion going to Europe.
However, from a demand proﬁle standpoint, natural gas looks
very different from oil: It’s extremely seasonal. Peak usage is
during the winter as the primary fuel for heating. Many
markets around the world rely on imported liqueﬁed natural
gas, or LNG, for their supply. When the Middle East conﬂict
began in March and supplies dropped, we saw a deferral of
buying activity. It was after the winter and a low-consumption
period of the year for natural gas, so there was no need to
draw down inventories.
But now, as we’re getting closer to the beginning of winter,
we have the lowest storage levels in decades in some key
markets, most notably Europe, which is creating upward price
pressure. If there is a cold winter, it could theoretically bring
Europe close to a full drawdown on storage.

Our medium-term outlook, though, is that once the Strait of
Hormuz reopens, most of the supply will return from Qatar,
which is the key exporter behind the strait. We estimate that
about 87% of capacity should come back online within one to
two months of the strait reopening.
In addition, a tremendous amount of LNG capacity is under
construction in North America, particularly in the US Gulf
Coast area. This addition should help bring market prices back
to normal relatively quickly over the course of 2027.
So, while low storage leaves LNG prone to further price
spikes over the next several months, we expect some price
moderation after the winter and a return to more historically
normal levels as we move through next year.
AP: Staying with natural gas, how meaningful is the demand
from AI and data centers today?
DM: As we see it, US natural gas has begun a new growth
cycle. The ﬁrst pillar of growth is LNG: Between 2025 and the
early 2030s, the US is expected to more than double its LNG
export capacity. And the second pillar is AI data centers,
whose demand for power—speciﬁcally electricity—currently
exceeds supply.
The US hasn’t seen material growth in electricity
consumption since the Great Financial Crisis. But growth
should accelerate rapidly with the development of AI data
centers across the US in the years ahead. We also expect a
little uptick from manufacturing and a broader, more
structural trend toward greater electriﬁcation—in appliances
and electric vehicles, for example—in the later part of the
decade.
Over the next 10 years, we think US electricity demand will
increase at a compound annual growth rate of 2.5%–3%. To
ﬁnd comparable growth, you would have to go back around
50 years. And relative to the no-growth market since the
ﬁnancial crisis, it is a material inﬂection higher.
Natural gas makes up about 40% of our electricity mix in the
US, and we think that share will hold roughly constant as the
size of the market grows rapidly. Putting it all together, we
think US natural gas demand will grow a little over 20%
between now and 2030.
AP: Midstream companies, which provide energy pipelines,
storage and other infrastructure, have seen strong earnings
and equity returns so far this year. Do you think that can last?
DM: Yes, growth is back for energy infrastructure in the US.
We came out of a world where energy demand growth
domestically was softer, and growth in US shale, which had
created a growth cycle for midstream companies in the
2010s, had slowed down.
Now, these companies have a new opportunity set, and in
response, they have increased capital spending, and key large
players have increased growth investment. A rerating of the
group has begun, tied in part to the repricing of the value of
existing cash ﬂows and incumbent infrastructure, and in part
to the uptick in future growth prospects.
In terms of how long it will last, we see this as a durable
structural growth cycle for natural gas that extends well
beyond this decade.
AP: Do you see the rise in interest rates as a potential
obstacle for midstream companies?
DM: We backtested midstream company performance over
the past several decades, and we did ﬁnd a linkage: When
interest rates increased, midstream tended to underperform
in a rate-hiking cycle, at least relative to the rest of energy.
But that underperformance tended to be short-lived, and
midstream companies typically outperformed once rates
stabilized.
So, dislocations due to a short-term move higher in rates
could actually create an attractive entry point in midstream
companies. And unlike the past ﬁve years, when they were
more steady-state businesses, the natural gas narrative and
the opportunity from AI should turn midstream companies
into much “growthier” businesses—more than just yield and
income plays.
AP: As you look across the energy chain, where else do you
see attractive opportunities? Are there any areas you think
investors may be overlooking?
DM: With the price of Brent sitting around $100 per barrel,
our recommendation is to rotate more defensively within the
space. Although energy stocks have not fully kept pace with
crude oil, they have had a good performance streak this year.
More defensive areas would include some of the oil majors,
pipeline companies—especially those focused on natural gas
—and select large cap service oil companies, whose growth in
free cash ﬂow doesn’t necessarily rely on commodity prices
staying at these elevated levels. While reﬁners are beneﬁtting
from the tightness in diesel supplies and we see upside
potential for earnings estimates, the stocks look more fully
valued to us currently. 
AP: Do you see any risks that might be underappreciated in
the energy space?
DM: Geopolitics are inherently uncertain, and we generally
advise against adding exposure to companies that have
already rallied substantially alongside geopolitical disruption
because it’s hard to predict the longevity of the move. We
suggest being opportunistic in energy stocks, adding on
pullbacks or looking for companies that are not directly
exposed to the situation in the Middle East, including those
involved in domestic gas infrastructure. ■

Glossary
Alpha is the excess return of an investment relative to the return of a benchmark index.
Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI) A ﬁeld of study that seeks to train computers to process large amounts of unstructured information in a manner
similar to human intelligence, capable of performing tasks such as learning and problem solving.
Beta is a measure of the volatility, or systematic risk, of a security or a portfolio in comparison to the market as a whole.
Correlation This is a statistical measure of how two securities move in relation to each other. This measure is often converted into what is
known as correlation coefﬁcient, which ranges between -1 and +1. Perfect positive correlation (a correlation coefﬁcient of +1) implies that as one
security moves, either up or down, the other security will move in lockstep, in the same direction. Alternatively, perfect negative correlation
means that if one security moves in either direction the security that is perfectly negatively correlated will move in the opposite direction. If the
correlation is 0, the movements of the securities are said to have no correlation; they are completely random. A correlation greater than 0.8 is
generally described as strong, whereas a correlation less than 0.5 is generally described as weak. 
Earnings revision breadth is deﬁned as the number of positive analyst revisions minus the number of negative analyst revisions divided by the
total number of revisions. 
Equity risk premium is the excess return that an individual stock or the overall stock market provides over a risk-free rate. The risk-free rate
represents the interest an investor would expect from an absolutely risk-free investment over a speciﬁed period of time.
Term premium is the excess yield that investors require to commit to holding a long-term bond instead of a series of shorter-term bonds.
Price to forward earnings calculates the price-to-earnings ratio that uses projected future earnings.

Bonds are subject to interest rate risk. When interest rates rise, bond prices fall; generally the longer a bond's maturity, the more sensitive it is
to this risk. Bonds may also be subject to call risk, which is the risk that the issuer will redeem the debt at its option, fully or partially, before
the scheduled maturity date. The market value of debt instruments may ﬂuctuate, and proceeds from sales prior to maturity may be more or
less than the amount originally invested or the maturity value due to changes in market conditions or changes in the credit quality of the issuer.
Bonds are subject to the credit risk of the issuer. This is the risk that the issuer might be unable to make interest and/or principal payments on a
timely basis. Bonds are also subject to reinvestment risk, which is the risk that principal and/or interest payments from a given investment may
be reinvested at a lower interest rate.

movements.  In general, as interest rates fall, prepayment speeds are likely to increase, thus shortening the MBS/CMO’s average life and likely
causing its market price to rise.  Conversely, as interest rates rise, prepayment speeds are likely to decrease, thus lengthening average life and
likely causing the MBS/CMO’s market price to fall. Some MBS/CMOs may have “original issue discount” (OID). OID occurs if the MBS/CMO’s

purposes, resulting in a tax liability even though interest was not received.  Investors are urged to consult their tax advisors for more

Duration, the most commonly used measure of bond risk, quantiﬁes the effect of changes in interest rates on the price of a bond or bond
portfolio. The longer the duration, the more sensitive the bond or portfolio would be to changes in interest rates. Generally, if interest rates
rise, bond prices fall and vice versa. Longer-term bonds carry a longer or higher duration than shorter-term bonds; as such, they would be
affected by changing interest rates for a greater period of time if interest rates were to increase. Consequently, the price of a long-term bond
would drop signiﬁcantly as compared to the price of a short-term bond.
