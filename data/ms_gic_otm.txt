INVESTMENT STRATEGY
As markets anniversary the fourth year of the equity bull market,
investors are dealing with the complexities of this transformational AI
investment cycle. Capex has continued apace, albeit at a peaking rate
of change, as demand for compute outstrips supply. But spending
increasingly relies on capital issuance, which has been accompanied by
wider credit spreads and higher costs of capital. Meanwhile, restraints
around timing of contract delivery are tightening amid surging
memory chip costs, power shortages and the local politics of data
centers.
We view these brakes placed on excessive exuberance as healthy and disciplined,
noting that they have fostered improved valuations. Certainly, no one likes to see
market pullbacks, and the Nasdaq Composite Index’s roughly 8% decline since its
Oct. 6 all-time high marks the third “mini correction” this year. But market action is
occurring against a solid backdrop, with leadership rotating and broadening. To wit,
the S&P 500 Index is down only 3.3% from its all-time closing high, and nearly 60%
of its components are above their 50-day moving average, while index proﬁt growth
is tracking at 32% year over year. Even more important, earnings forecasts are still
trending higher on better-than-expected economic growth, investment,
consumption and manufacturing activity. The US Treasury market is also
cooperating again, as extreme oversold conditions have given way to modest
retracement. The 10-year yield, having climbed to a 24-year high, has declined
approximately 40 basis points. This has come amid growing conﬁdence that the
Warsh-led Federal Reserve will move in a data-driven manner and at a measured
pace, and that inﬂation, while sticky, will not become unanchored from long-term
expectations.
Against this backdrop, risks around both domestic politics and geopolitics continue
to loom large, necessitating recalibration of expectations. That said, this is when
diversiﬁed portfolios should shine, as we transition from a “rising tide lifts all boats”
scenario to increased bifurcation between winners and losers. Stay the course, and
rebalance to strategic asset allocation targets. ■

GLOBAL MACRO
Elections Near and Far
Seth B. Carpenter, Global Chief Economist and Head of Macro
Research, Morgan Stanley & Co. LLC
Elections have consequences, as the saying goes, and markets
are weighing the consequences of elections past, present and
future. The recent election in Brazil and the elections in
France next year put ﬁscal policy front and center. In the UK,
markets have been focused on the ﬁrst budget under a new
prime minister. The US midterms are both informed by and
will likely inform domestic policies and geopolitics. Each
election has interesting implications.
The US midterm elections take place this week. Polls point to
divided government, and if that outcome materializes, we
expect the macro impulse from federal policy to remain
broadly unchanged. Tariffs, trade and immigration have all
generated uncertainty over time, but these are areas where
the executive branch exercises authority without needing new
legislation. A change in control of Congress most likely will
not bring changes in how those powers are exercised.
Although AI has become a political topic, we do not think
federal policies will change materially, so the election results
will likely only hint at state and local involvement in
permitting, access to the grid and electricity pricing, and
support for intervention does not fall neatly along party lines.
But with a big enough swing in House and Senate majorities,
we do think some domestic spending cuts could be reversed,
adding a modest boost to growth, and military spending
might be restrained, pushing in the other direction.
Markets have clearly anticipated a much larger shift in the
case of Brazil. Lula da Silva’s election was followed by
substantial market volatility from a reassessment of ﬁscal
prospects. But our Brazil team had highlighted before the
election that stabilizing the ﬁscal outlook would be difﬁcult
regardless of the winner. More than 91% of spending is
mandatory, so that meaningful ﬁscal adjustment requires
Congress, not just the president, to act.
Politics aside, we expect the Brazilian economy to face a
moderately restrictive credit cycle next year, which the
Central Bank of Brazil has begun to highlight in
communication but we suspect the market has not fully
appreciated. So while the election matters for speciﬁc ﬁscal
policies, we do not think it has fundamentally changed the
underlying economic risks.
France’s elections are still some ways off but are in focus
because of the developments in the government bond (OATs)
market and the protests across that country. Higher
borrowing costs make difﬁcult ﬁscal arithmetic even harder,
and growth is not sufﬁcient to offset those costs.
Negotiations for the 2027 budget will likely come in late
November and December. Current proposals would merely
stabilize the deﬁcit. Higher taxes could inhibit growth,
offsetting at least some of the ﬁscal narrowing. Spending cuts
tend to be politically difﬁcult to deliver and also risk slowing
growth. The choices appear difﬁcult heading into the election.
For markets, one key question is whether we would see
spillovers. Spain, Greece and Italy could all face election-
related risks in 2027, broadening volatility in sovereign debt
markets. Broader ﬁnancial friction could prompt an ECB
response, but that outcome is not in our baseline.
So where does this leave us? In the US, markets have
relatively high conviction about the political outcome, but the
economic and market implications are either modest or
unclear. In Brazil, an unexpected political outcome generated
volatility, but we have always said the ﬁscal situation
depended less on the election than markets believed, and the
credit cycle has not been fully priced. In France, political
uncertainty is high, but the ﬁscal challenges are already clear,
regardless of who ultimately governs.
Elections usually have consequences, but sorting them out is
not always straightforward. The US midterms are this week.
Polls suggest a divided government, and if they are right, our
base case for the macro impulse from federal policy is very
little change in the status quo. For other countries, the ﬁscal
consequences are likely greater, and that is part of what is
driving markets. ■

US ECONOMICS
Trick or Trend: Is Growth Outpacing
Restraint?
Michael T. Gapen, Chief US Economist, Morgan Stanley & Co. LLC
Despite resilient growth and strong spending, there
is little evidence of overheating. If growth becomes
more demand-driven, however, the case for
additional rate hikes would likely strengthen.
The ﬁnal week of October highlighted the main tension in the
monetary-policy outlook: Growth in economic activity
remains resilient, raising doubts about how quickly—some
might say “whether”—inﬂation will return to the Federal
Reserve’s 2% target. Growth accelerated in the third quarter,
household spending was robust and business investment
continued to surge. While the Fed opted to leave rates
unchanged at its Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)
meeting, there was little in the week’s data to suggest its task
is done.
The question is how much monetary-policy restraint to
provide. The answer, in our view, depends on the source of
growth. Demand and supply shocks both argue for higher real
rates, but demand-driven growth would likely require higher
nominal and real rates to deliver on the inﬂation mandate. To
date, we ﬁnd little evidence of overheating and forecast a
measured pace of policy-rate ﬁrming: We expect 25-basis-
point hikes in December and March, implying a terminal
target range of 4.25% to 4.5%. If the labor market were to
begin tightening, our view would likely shift toward more
hikes.
US GDP: ANOTHER STRONG QUARTER. Third-quarter GDP
growth accelerated to 3.4%, with the expansion continuing to
be investment-led (see chart). Business spending on
equipment remained exceptionally strong, reﬂecting ongoing
AI-related capex and a broader investment cycle that has so
far proved resilient to higher rates and geopolitics. Equipment
spending rose at a 20.9% annualized pace in the quarter,
boosted by a 37.3% increase in information processing
equipment. Spending on data centers was up 127%.
The consumer also pitched in, despite elevated energy prices
and slow growth in real income. Real personal consumption
spending rose 3.8% for the second consecutive quarter.
Services rose 4.1%, while strength in goods moderated
somewhat, rising 3.0%. The slowdown came in durable goods,
which rose 2.3%.
The September personal income and spending data reinforces
our view that consumption will likely moderate in coming
quarters. Personal spending rose 0.5%, while personal income
increased 0.2%, pushing the savings rate down to 3.8%. The
decline in the savings rate suggests households are still
willing to spend despite elevated prices and borrowing costs,
which likely indicates continued support from asset markets
in sustaining demand. However, household spending is
running faster than income, suggesting that the consumer
cannot indeﬁnitely maintain the recent pace of growth by
drawing further down on savings. Household spending is
likely to remain resilient but at a more sustainable pace of
spending. This likely points to some slowing ahead.

SUPPLY OR DEMAND GROWTH? The immediate question
facing the Fed is whether inﬂation is moving in the right
direction quickly enough, with the risk being whether it is
moving in the right direction. The ultimate answer, in our
view, depends on the sources of US growth. If growth
resilience is primarily supply-side-driven, then appropriate
policy still calls for higher rates, but the Fed can probably
hike more slowly and may end at a lower terminal rate than
feared. Supply-driven growth can keep inﬂation on a
downward path while limiting overheating. This is the
“measured recalibration” of monetary policy embedded in our
baseline forecast. Our decomposition of potential growth into
its component parts continues to point to faster growth in
output per hour, consistent with a signiﬁcant supply-side
impulse.
However, if demand forces begin to dominate because AI
spending spills into other parts of the economy, then
demand-side price pressures could replace prior supply-side
factors, preventing inﬂation from decelerating—or worse,
causing inﬂation to ﬁrm. This outcome would necessitate
higher nominal and real rates than we anticipate, as
appropriate monetary policy would require more restriction
to moderate demand and put inﬂation on a downward trend.
How do we know the difference between supply- and
demand-driven growth? Conventional economics would say
to look at the unemployment rate as a high-frequency, broad-
based measure of resource utilization. If the AI-investment

think, e.g., greater than 10%. ■

US EQUITIES
Autonomous Trucking: Time to Get
Real
Ravi Shankar, Equity Analyst, Morgan Stanley & Co. LLC
After a decade of technology development, the
debate around autonomous trucking has shifted to
commercialization. As we enter the ﬁnal 12–18
months before the start of serial production, it’s
time for key stakeholders to agree on major
aspects of how it will all work.
When we initiated coverage of the trucking industry in 2026,
one of the pillars of our constructive thesis was the
transformative potential of autonomous trucking. While the
path has been a winding one—plenty has transpired as we
anticipated, and even more has played out differently—we
believe we’ve arrived at a key inﬂection point. In our view, the
debate around the technological aspects has been settled, as
questions regarding regulation and scale have also been
addressed. With that, we now approach a ﬁnal hurdle: Can it
be commercialized?
In some ways, the commercialization hurdle is the easiest to
clear—effectively the dotting of i’s and crossing of t’s for a
technology already shown to work in select use-cases. But it’s
also tricky, because for the ﬁrst time in the technology’s 10-
year history, the burden of proof and execution has shifted
from the autonomous tech companies to more conservative
stakeholders in the value chain. Understandably, many of
them do not want the pursuit of big gains from a new
technology to disrupt their existing models of service
reliability, unit economics and liability.
TACKLING THE QUESTIONS. As such, we see the next 12
months as a critical period when all four groups of
stakeholders—tech providers, original equipment
manufacturers (OEMs), carriers/ﬂeet operators and shippers
—need to hash out commercial deployment details and
answer basic questions about how this is all going to work.
Some of the friction points may be resolved by more
transparent communication, and some still need to be
investigated, but doing so should help put the industry on
track toward its ultimate goal: the start of serial commercial
production in 2027.
Notably, the unprecedented supply-side squeeze, which has
already pushed our truckload freight index to a record, is a
reminder of the structural driver shortage and wage inﬂation
that the US economy faces if it maintains its current
trajectory. There appear to be very few sustainable solutions
to these issues outside of autonomous trucks, which should
bring critical demand-pull for the technology, in addition to
supply-push—a big difference versus the passenger robotaxi
space, where we consider the demand-pull to be determined.
In an effort to kickstart the communication process, we
surveyed 146 carriers and shippers regarding their views on
autonomous trucking. We also interviewed ﬁrms across
multiple areas of the Morgan Stanley & Co. Research
coverage universe. The results reveal three main areas of
uncertainty restraining ﬂeets from fully embracing the
promise of autonomous trucks: 1) network coverage/number
of operable lanes; 2) conﬁrmation of unit economics (see
charts), especially the potential need for ﬁrst- and last-mile
drivers at both ends of a highway move; and 3) general
maintenance, reliability and warranty questions.
The ﬁrst and third issues are considered surmountable, and
ﬂeets believe they will be resolved with more work and
information, perhaps as early as the start of serial commercial
production in 2027. The second, however, is viewed as a
potentially more fatal problem, albeit one we think is
misunderstood. For example, around 60% of survey
respondents said that less than 10% of their freight would be
compatible with autonomous trucking if there were the need
to use ﬁrst- and last-mile drivers at both ends of highway
drop-off points, despite one-third of carriers and shippers
indicating that at least 75% of line-haul miles are point to
point and on-highway within their network.
However, according to our proprietary AlphaWise geolocation
analysis, which looked at 10,000-plus major commercial
locations across more than 100 of the largest commercial
shippers in the US, the average facility is approximately 2.6
miles from the nearest highway ramp point (and around one
mile from the nearest main road ramp point). Furthermore,
autonomous trucks can drive at least seven to 10 miles on
surface streets, which many ﬂeet operators may not realize.
Finally, our updated total-cost-of-ownership (TCO) analysis
shows that even if ﬁrst-/last-mile drivers are needed at both
ends, autonomous trucks’ unit economics would be around
11% cheaper than in the case of human drivers today, with
signiﬁcant utilization gains on top of that. Other friction
points, including the need for local drivers for yard
movement, treatment at weight scales and fuel stations, can
largely be solved with technology.
INVESTMENT IMPLICATIONS. We see investors increasingly
focusing on the stock implications here. In fact, autonomous
trucking has been a top-three incoming client topic for us this
year. Our total addressable market (TAM) assumptions show
that the opportunity remains signiﬁcant, ranging from $0.5
billion to $1.1 trillion in the US alone, while our TCO analysis
indicates that anticipated return on invested capital (ROIC)
remains very attractive (approximately 20% net cost savings,
meaning an autonomous ﬂeet should be roughly 7.5 times as
proﬁtable as a human-driven ﬂeet today).

Apart from the direct implications for autonomous tech
providers, investors are focused on second- and third-
derivative winners among the other stakeholders, like truck
OEMs, carriers and shippers, as well as on potentially
challenged verticals like railroads and freight brokers, where
autonomous trucking could present signiﬁcant risk over time.
Even if the ramp-up is slow and steady, the market is likely to
extrapolate its trajectory to the end-destination and reﬂect
that in price/earnings multiples for the winners and risk-
exposed companies in the short term.
Despite the growing investor interest, unlike other areas of
tech disruption in non-tech industries (especially AI),
transportation companies are playing their cards very close to
the chest for the time being. Some of the reticence to talk
about future beneﬁts stems from the risk of potentially
alienating existing driver bases in the near term, especially
when the truck market is so tight. While this is
understandable, it is merely kicking the can down the road,
because the labor issue during the transition period will need
to be addressed eventually. We think management teams are
waiting for the commercial production launch of these trucks
before speaking out about potential for transformation, given
the risk of being an evangelist too early if there are more
delays. This “bird-in-hand” approach also applies to
communications with investors and the equity market.
Some of the reluctance to go public is also driven by
competitive factors, as companies look to establish an initial
beachhead, especially given what is likely to be very limited
supply of autonomous trucks in the early years. ■

Data Center Capacity: Tracking the
Shortfall
Cameron McVeigh, CFA, Equity Analyst, Morgan Stanley & Co. LLC
Our updated forecast for global data center
capacity, featuring a 23% compound annual growth
rate, implies a 49-gigawatt power shortfall by
2028. It also suggests greater opportunity for well-
positioned players in the AI-infrastructure build-
out. 
Amid evolving debates around the unprecedented AI-capex
build-out and potential changes to its pace and scale, we
recently updated our data center capacity models. The
analysis meaningfully informs our outlook regarding supply
and demand dynamics, supporting our conclusion that
demand for compute is likely to signiﬁcantly exceed supply
for years to come.
Leveraging a bottom-up build by operator, along with a top-
down capacity forecast, we now expect a 23% compound
annual growth rate (CAGR) for global data center
information  technology (IT) capacity between 2026 and
2030. In our base case, we expect approximately 180
gigawatts (GW) of IT capacity to come online by 2030, for a
total of 316 GW (IT). 
We note that our GW (IT) forecasts refer to power delivered
directly to computing hardware and that GW (utility) refers
to total gross electricity pulled from the power grid to run
the entire data center. Matched against expected demand, our
forecasts imply a 49-GW (utility) global shortfall by 2028,
based on 327 GW (utility) of data center demand versus 278
GW (utility) of supply (see chart).

INVESTMENT IMPLICATIONS. The accelerating demand for
compute is accompanied by increasingly difﬁcult conditions to
bring capacity online, given tight supply chains, inﬂating labor
costs and access to capital. That said, we remain bullish on
companies enabling the AI-infrastructure build-out,
particularly “time-to-power” providers—ﬁrms that focus on
reducing the time it takes to connect customers such as AI
data centers to a reliable electricity source.
Within our data center operator/developer coverage, we
expect colocation data centers to perform well as cost
inﬂation and supply constraints, including moratoriums and
permitting delays, should further strengthen pricing power.
Colocation’s smaller facilities and critical functionality offer
greater insulation from community opposition than large AI
campuses. In their typically smaller facilities, third-party
colocation data centers provide power, cooling and related
services for other ﬁrms’ servers and networking equipment.    
We see outsized beneﬁts of the supply-constrained
environment accruing to established colocation operators
with scale and access to power. In addition, open-weight
model-share gains should beneﬁt colocation data centers as
enterprises look to deploy their own AI models on their
private infrastructure. We expect the percent of companies
using open-weight models to roughly double over the next
three years, with the retail and tech sectors potentially
experiencing the largest increases.
SURVEY SUPPORT. Our favorable stance is supported by
results from Morgan Stanley & Co.’s proprietary AlphaWise
surveys. In particular, we note that the average price for data
center capacity contracts signed in the past three months has
risen by 11% versus a year ago. Nearly all respondents
reported price increases and 56% cited gains above 10%,
suggesting sustained pricing strength alongside expectations
for continued capacity demand growth. 
There has also been a rising trend toward preleasing capacity,
with the average respondent preleasing 43% in advance.
Results indicate that hyperscaler cloud-service providers and
enterprise customers have the highest percent of capacity
preleased. ■

Tech Momentum Volatility Surges
Factor volatility has characterized 2026. Given large
movements in “AI beneﬁciaries” and the “AI disrupted,”
the momentum factor—a measure of price strength—has
been especially volatile. This has been most acute in tech,
where investors have sharply distinguished between
semiconductors and software. The Morgan Stanley US
TMT Momentum Basket—a group of tech stocks with
strong price trends—has experienced almost twice as
many 5%-plus daily moves as at the peak of the 2000
tech bubble. Long- and short-momentum baskets have
become increasingly negatively correlated: They have
moved more than 1% in opposite directions on 28 trading
days this year—over four times the average since 2022.
This matters because hedge funds often pair long
positions with shorts.—Kevin Demers, CFA
Brazilian Equities: Oil—Friend or Foe?
In the week after Brazil’s presidential runoff, the MSCI Brazil
Index fell nearly 24% in US dollar terms—its worst week in
almost 26 years, excluding the Great Financial Crisis. Of the
six elections since 2002, three produced above-average
returns over the following six months. Two of the three
disappointments—2014 and 2022—coincided with falling oil
prices. Brazilian equities have historically been correlated
with oil, reﬂecting their energy exposure. Since the Iran
conﬂict erupted in February, however, that relationship has
turned sharply negative, reaching its weakest level in over
two decades. This suggests investors fear inﬂation could
delay Brazilian rate cuts and pressure equity multiples.
Policy clarity may support a rebound, but normalized oil
prices could help, too.—Alfredo Pinel, CAIA

Lenders accept bitcoin as collateral, while research

sizes. As ﬁnancial ﬁrms try to ﬁgure out where bitcoin ﬁts,

helping offset these macro headwinds, suggesting the
precious metal is becoming less dependent on a supportive
rates environment.

Global gold ETFs recorded their strongest quarterly inﬂows

GOLD AND BOND YIELDS. Rising rates and bond yields
typically weigh on gold because they can make bonds more
attractive to investors than non-interest-bearing assets like
gold. But this is not always the case: The reason yields are
rising matters.
Following September’s Fed hike, US short-term yields eased
and long-term yields resumed their rise, steepening the
Treasury yield curve. As the US ﬁscal deﬁcit approaches $2
trillion, growing debt issuance and competition from long-
dated AI-related corporate borrowing are pressuring  long-
term yields, potentially reﬂecting investors’ desire for greater
compensation for taking greater risk. As the World Gold
Council noted in its September report on ETF ﬂows, this can
reﬂect "concerns over inﬂation uncertainty, ﬁscal
sustainability and sovereign bond market risks." Such
concerns, in turn, could encourage diversiﬁcation toward gold,
which carries no issuer or credit risk. This may already be
playing out, with ETF inﬂows remaining strong despite rising
yields.
In a speech on Oct. 5, Bundesbank President Joachim Nagel
made a similar argument in favor of central bank gold buying:
"The recent rise in global government bond yields has
boosted the relative attractiveness of debt securities again. At
the same time, rising debt levels have increased concerns
about the credit risk of these assets. Furthermore,
geopolitical risks are likely to continue to shape reserve
management decisions. Taken together, the case for further
diversiﬁcation into gold remains signiﬁcant." We note that
Germany holds the world’s second-largest gold reserves.
IMPACT OF RATE EXPECTATIONS. Falling rates and bond
yields typically reduce the opportunity cost of holding gold
and can encourage renewed ETF inﬂows and speculative
buying—and we would expect that to happen if rate
expectations ease going forward. MS & Co. Research
economists expect just two more Fed hikes through the end
of 2027, compared with around 2.5 hikes priced by the
market. If inﬂation pressures ease, allowing the Fed to deliver
fewer hikes than the market expects, this could support ETF
inﬂows into gold. The MS & Co. economics team also sees the
US 10-year Treasury yield reaching 4.7% by the third quarter
of 2027, from around 5% currently.
GOLD AND OIL PRICES. Gold has come under pressure as oil
prices have rallied, particularly since late August: Brent crude
has risen from $90 to $115 per barrel, while gold has fallen
from $4,650 to $3,925 per ounce. In the near term, a jump in
oil prices might bring further downside pressure to gold,
particularly if it is used as a source of liquidity.
However, we think investors should consider buying the dips
in gold, even if oil prices stay higher for longer. A sustained
period of elevated oil prices would raise the risk of
stagﬂation, with energy costs feeding through to broader
inﬂation, while squeezing household incomes, business
margins and growth. As growth weakens, central banks may
ﬁnd it increasingly difﬁcult to justify further tightening, and a
pause in rate hikes while inﬂation stays elevated should
support gold, all else equal.
A stagﬂation environment also tends to weigh on equities and
bonds together, which strengthens gold’s role as a portfolio
diversiﬁer. Stagﬂation has historically been associated with
periods of strong gold performance.
A BRIGHT OUTLOOK. Putting all this together, we see gold
showing resilience against an increasingly challenging macro
backdrop, supported by strong central bank demand and ETF
inﬂows. We think this reﬂects growing appetite for gold as a
hedge against geopolitical risks and concerns over
government debt sustainability. With prices repeatedly
ﬁnding support around $4,000 per ounce, we see an
attractive risk-reward scenario from here and target $5,000
by the second quarter of next year.
Lower yields offer the clearest route higher for gold. Falling
bond yields, oil prices or both could ease Fed tightening
expectations, reduce gold’s opportunity cost and encourage
renewed investment inﬂows. However, further increases in
long-term yields need not be bearish for the precious metal if
driven by ﬁscal sustainability concerns, encouraging
diversiﬁcation toward real assets like gold, which has no
credit risk. Similarly, while rising oil prices could initially
weigh on gold through higher rate expectations, sustained
strength in oil could eventually shift market concerns toward
growth and stagﬂation, creating an alternative source of
support. ■

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE
AI Financing: The Quality Divide
Vishwanath Tirupattur, Chief Fixed Income Strategist and Director of
Quantitative Research, Morgan Stanley & Co. LLC
Vishwas Patkar, Head of US Corporate Credit Strategy, Morgan Stanley & Co.
LLC
Sharply higher rates and credit spreads have
recently raised the question of whether the
increased cost of borrowing threatens the AI build-
out. We think it will proceed apace, but with
ﬁnancing terms increasingly shaped by credit
strength, collateral and structure.
AI infrastructure, and how it gets ﬁnanced, has been the
deﬁning market theme of 2026, particularly in credit. The
sheer scale of investment has drawn in hyperscalers,
semiconductor makers, data center developers, neoclouds
(cloud-computing service providers) and virtually every
channel of public and private credit. Just as the need for
capital is accelerating, higher interest rates and wider spreads
are raising borrowing costs. Here, we analyze what elevated
borrowing costs could mean for the pace of investment and
the volume and composition of credit issuance needed.
Our core premise remains unchanged: Demand for compute
vastly exceeds supply, and that imbalance is likely to persist
for years. Combined with demonstrable progress in AI
adoption and monetization, this imbalance continues to drive
AI-infrastructure-capex projections higher. Our equity
research colleagues now estimate that total capex for the
four largest hyperscalers will rise by more than 50% in 2027
versus 2026. These projections reﬂect strong conviction that
the highest-quality players in the AI ecosystem have a line of
sight to returns on invested capital (ROIC) of 25% or more.
Against that return potential, the question is whether
borrowing costs have risen enough to materially alter the
economics of the next dollar of AI investment.
HIGH-QUALITY HYPERSCALERS. The answer varies across
the AI ecosystem. First consider the four high-quality
hyperscalers—Alphabet, Amazon, Meta and Microsoft. With
average credit ratings of roughly AA, average market
capitalizations exceeding $3 trillion, diversiﬁed businesses and
long track records of proﬁtability, they have ample capacity
to issue more debt. Their high initial ratings also provide a
meaningful cushion, creating additional balance-sheet
ﬂexibility. Weighed against prospective ROIC of 25%-plus,
higher borrowing costs alone are unlikely to materially alter
their commitment to AI-related capex.
For these issuers, we expect price to adjust before quantity:
with wider spreads but not meaningfully lower issuance. This
distinction matters for the aggregate outlook. As these four
hyperscalers directly or indirectly account for more than 75%
of expected AI-related capex, we think a rise in borrowing
costs from here is unlikely to meaningfully slow the bulk of
the AI build-out.
MOVING DOWN THE QUALITY SPECTRUM. The calculus
changes materially further down in quality. Lower-rated
hyperscalers, neoclouds and other participants lack the
ratings cushion, market capitalization and, in many cases, the
long track records of proﬁtability of the higher-quality
players. These vulnerabilities have surfaced in recent weeks,
as some issuers have faced more limited capital market access
and sharp declines in their bond prices. For them, higher
funding costs can become a binding constraint. As borrowing
costs rise, both AI-related capex and the credit issuance
required to ﬁnance it from lower-rated players are likely to
become more sensitive to price.
MARKETS STARTING TO DIFFERENTIATE. Through the recent
volatility, the credit markets have been notably discerning.
Sharp price declines have been concentrated in unsecured AI-
related debt from lower-quality issuers, while the reaction in
loans secured by chips or lease payments from high-quality
hyperscalers has been far more contained. At the same time,
unsecured bond spreads for the highest-quality hyperscalers
have remained relatively stable—unlike over the summer
when they led the widening. The market is differentiating not
only among issuers, but also by credit quality, collateral and
ﬁnancing structure.
This differentiation has two important implications. First,
lower-quality issuers may increasingly need to rely more on
secured, asset-level ﬁnancing. Second, accessing that
ﬁnancing may require greater credit support from the highest-
quality participants in the AI ecosystem, including highly rated
semiconductor companies, through contractual commitments,
backstops or other forms of support. Higher funding costs,
therefore, may not just affect how much these issuers invest
in capex but also reshape how that investment is ﬁnanced.
THE NEW DIVIDE. The bottom line is that higher borrowing
costs are unlikely to derail the AI infrastructure build-out, but
they will increasingly determine who ﬁnances it and how. For
the highest-quality participants, strong balance sheets, ratings
ﬂexibility and prospective returns well above borrowing costs
mean that price is likely to adjust before quantity.
Further down the credit spectrum, higher funding costs can
constrain both capex and market access, forcing borrowers
into secured structures and greater reliance on support from
stronger counterparties. As the AI capital cycle matures,
credit quality, collateral and structure will matter more, not
less. The capital will be there, but increasingly on different
terms for different borrowers. ■

What Would It Take to Unlock the
US Housing Market?
After the Federal Reserve raised interest rates in September,
the 30-year mortgage rate climbed above 7%. Combined with
high home prices and the lowest housing turnover in decades,
higher mortgage rates have cast a pall over the US housing
market. “Early this year, we were being asked what mortgage
rate would unlock the housing market. Now we’re being
asked what rate would break it,” notes James Egan, the US
housing strategist for  Morgan Stanley & Co. Research. He
recently discussed the market’s complexities and his outlook
with Morgan Stanley Wealth Management investment
strategist Sarah Wolfe. Below is an edited version of their
conversation on Oct. 26.
Sarah Wolfe (SW): Let’s start with mortgage rates. We saw
the 30-year rate dip below 6% back in February and home
affordability improve to its highest level since 2022. Now
mortgage rates are above 7%, and the monthly payment on
the median-price home has risen 15%. What’s driving up
mortgage rates and where do you think they’re headed?
James Egan (JE): There are a lot of components to the
mortgage rate, but two stand out currently. The ﬁrst is the
selloff in US Treasury bonds between the ﬁrst quarter of this
year and now.  At one point, the ﬁve-year Treasury yield was
up 150 basis points, and the 10-year was up 125 basis points.
So, that was the biggest driver.
Another component on the investment side is wider spreads
on mortgage-backed securities. We have gone from a market
that was pricing in multiple Fed rate cuts to one that’s now
pricing in several more hikes. Mortgage investors are
inherently short rate volatility, and amid this backdrop they
want to be compensated for the added risk.
In terms of where we’re going from here, volatility could
decline if the market coalesces around a path for Fed rates,
and then we could see some improvement in mortgage-
backed security spreads—but if that happens, the
improvement might only be in the neighborhood of a quarter
point. For any material drop in mortgage rates, the ﬁve- and
10-year part of the Treasury yield curve would need to move
lower.
SW: Higher mortgage rates appear to have brought the
housing market to a standstill:  Purchase applications were
down 11% year over year, existing home sales look depressed,
and new housing inventory has risen to more than eight
months of supply. Do you expect activity to pick up?
JE: If mortgage rates stay where they are, we expect more
softness in sales volumes. We already saw a pullback in the
third quarter. However, there is a certain amount of housing
activity that has to occur regardless of where mortgage rates
are and where affordability is—call it the “life happens” rate.
For instance, some number of people will need to move for
work reasons. We have been near this “ﬂoor” of activity for
some time, and I don’t anticipate much change now that
affordability has fallen again.
Lack of affordability has been constraining demand for
housing since 2022 and 2023, when mortgage rates rose to
more than 8% and home-price growth hit a record. By the
fourth quarter of 2023, existing home sales volume as a
percentage of the housing stock fell to the lowest level in 40
years. Although affordability has improved since then, existing
home sales have not really recovered—they’ve been
bouncing along that 40-year low. There was an uptick in late
2025 and early 2026, but there wasn’t a true breakout in
demand.
So, looking at the latest rise in mortgage rates, we frame it
this way: The probability of upside risk—much stronger sales
volumes than expected—continues to come down. However,
the probability of dramatically weaker sales has not gone up
because we have already reached that activity ﬂoor.
SW: Could you break down the existing home versus the new
home market?
JE: Existing and new home sales are incredibly different
markets right now. What’s driven the wedge between them is
the “lock-in” effect in the existing home space.
Essentially, a homeowner who purchased or reﬁnanced their
home before 2022 locked in a much lower mortgage rate. So
they are not incentivized to sell their home, pay off that
mortgage and take out a new one at a much higher rate to
buy a much more expensive house. To put some numbers
around that, the effective rate on the outstanding mortgage
universe is around 4.3% versus the recent prevailing rate—
about 7.4%.
News headlines make it sound like affordability has
deteriorated for all homeowners. But in reality, if you bought
your home in 2016 and reﬁnanced in 2020 or 2021 at record-
low mortgage rates, and your income has been growing at the
same pace as median household income, your monthly
mortgage payment as a share of your income is probably
around 7%. But if you’re buying a home today for the ﬁrst
time, your monthly payment as a share of your income is
probably closer to 25%. That’s what’s driven existing home
inventories down and protected existing home prices.
In the new home market, the lock-in effect does not exist
because there is no in-place homeowner beholden to a lower
mortgage rate. Single-family home-starts climbed throughout
2020 and 2021 to local highs. Units under construction
swelled, peaking in 2023, and now these new homes are
completed and available for sale—in fact, new home

inventory is the highest since 2008. To move the inventory,
builders are incentivizing potential homebuyers by offering
“rate buy-downs,” in which a homebuyer gets a lower
mortgage rate and thus a more affordable monthly payment.
Today, though, even with these incentives, new home prices
have been falling and are below existing home prices for the
ﬁrst time in decades.
SW: Where are home prices at the national level? Is there a
scenario where we actually see home price depreciation?
JE: Median new home prices are down—but that is very
different from saying that home prices overall are down. In
fact, as mortgage rates have increased, listing growth for
existing homes has slowed. With fewer existing homes on the
market, national home price appreciation has increased
recently from 70 or 80 basis points year over year to 1.9%.
While home prices are coming down in some areas, to see
them truly depreciate nationally, you need motivated sellers.
And we struggle to ﬁnd that. Mortgage lending standards and
the quality of mortgage products have remained so robust
that we don’t foresee delinquencies, defaults or foreclosures
getting to the point where homeowners would need to sell
and national home prices would depreciate as a result.
Could we see things get softer as affordability remains this
challenged? Sure, and we will be closely watching existing
home listings for signs that homeowners are becoming more
willing to sell.
SW: How do geographic differences ﬁt in? The Northeast and
the Midwest, for example, have been seeing the strongest
price gains, whereas areas in the Sunbelt, Florida and Texas
have had more inventory growth and weaker prices. Do you
expect that dynamic to continue?
JE: The short answer is yes.
As we look at different places, we have found one variable
that is highly correlated to home-price performance: how
many homes are listed for sale right now versus how many
were listed for sale in 2019. Back then, before anybody knew
what COVID was, listed inventory was close to historic lows.
But then the pandemic happened, the lock-in effect became
more severe than ever and listed inventory plummeted to an
all-time low. While listed inventory has increased, it’s still 17%
below those historic lows in 2019 on a national basis.
However, if you look at big metropolitan areas in Texas,
Florida and other parts of the Southeast, listed inventories
are up 20% to 50% versus 2019. And looking at places in
Connecticut, New York, Pennsylvania and Ohio, inventory
levels are 45% to 70% below where they were in 2019. That’s
driven a material difference in pricing.
In a world in which affordability pressures are going to keep
demand soft, those inventory differentials are going to
matter: They still speak to outperformance in the Northeast
and the Midwest versus the Southeast.
SW: Switching to the supply side, what is the environment
like for homebuilders?
JE: From a holistic standpoint, inﬂation, tariffs, the higher cost
of inputs and labor shortages have been issues in the home
building space for some time. Homebuilders are also facing
increased pressure from the regulatory side. And now, on top
of all that, there is eight and a half months of supply.
We could talk about which headwind is the most or least
surmountable, but I think the important point is that there
are so many headwinds right now. It’s weighed on
homebuilder conﬁdence, which is the lowest in 14 years.
In our view, until we get affordability improvement that can
clear the backlog of homes for sale and see a path toward a
healthier demand environment that would then incentivize
more building, it’s going to be a challenging backdrop for
housing starts and for building volumes.
SW: Do you see policy levers that could alleviate the lack of
affordability effectively—for example, mortgage product
innovation or checks for ﬁrst-time homebuyers?
JE: The housing market is a very complicated mechanism, and
so it’s very difﬁcult to ﬁnd a silver bullet that ﬁxes everything.
Checks to ﬁrst-time homebuyers, for example, would certainly
increase sales volumes and home prices. But in our view, it
would simply pull demand forward, and affordability would
deteriorate as that happened. So, it would be a short-term
beneﬁt for medium- or longer-term issues.
A different mortgage product could help. One reason for the
lock-in effect is that the housing market is so over-indexed to
30-year ﬁxed-rate mortgages. Over 90% of the outstanding
mortgage universe, by balance or by count, is ﬁxed-rate right
now. We’ve started to see an increase in adjustable-rate
mortgage (ARM) applications, and we think that a larger,
more liquid ARM market would help with affordability on the
margins.
Also, we recognize that nationally, housing supply is low, and
more building volume would help with affordability. But there
are regulatory hurdles and rules on what can be built where.
Although some proposals in the Road to Housing Act passed
earlier this year attempt to incentivize more building, they are
going to take time to implement. So, this is not something
that can change overnight. ■

Global Equities Weight Relative toModel Benchmark  
US Overweight
The productivity-boom thesis catalyzed by the GenAI-capex build-out, along with aggressive
ﬁscal and regulatory stimulus, has become very much consensus. Forecasts are ambitious and
embed an expectation for cyclical broadening that takes "S&P 493" earnings growth from 6%–
8% in 2025 to 24%–26% in 2026. In a market that is already expensive, concentrated and
complacent, this suggests the aperture for upside surprise is narrow, and markets are apt to be
quite brittle in the face of exogenous shocks. With midterm-election-related policy already
hitting a fever pitch, potential for these undiscounted events to occur is rising. Thus, while we
are bullish (with MS & Co.'s 2Q2027 S&P 500 Index target at 8,300), we prefer stock-picking
to simply owning the cap-weighted index. Favor ﬁnancials, health care, select industrials and
energy.
International Equities  (Developed
Markets) Underweight
Fiscal stimulus, a response to the "America First" agenda, has helped to drive recent
outperformance. This is creating rest-of-world (ROW) opportunities that may simultaneously
enjoy monetary, ﬁscal and currency-related stimulus. The outlook is improving in Japan, as
corporate restructuring occurs alongside a reﬂationary surge three decades in the making.
Emerging Markets Overweight
The "new Monroe Doctrine" creates opportunities not only for pro-business political stability in
Latin America, but also for direct investment. For Asia, strategic realignment opportunities
continue to surface, as the US turns away and the US-China trade conﬂict has effectively
reached a one-year truce, fostering opportunities. India remains a recommended secular
growth long. The GIC reduced its oveweight after a signiﬁncant rally in semiconductor names,
which skewed index composition.
Global Fixed Income Weight Relative toModel Benchmark  
US Investment Grade Underweight
The GIC maintains close-to-neutral views in investment grade. In the ﬁrst half of 2026, solid
growth, supported by ﬁscal stimulus, and inﬂationary pressures supported a higher-for-longer
interest rate environment. Should the Fed maintain a "hold" versus enacting a hike as its next
move, investors could ﬁnd some value in IG. As a point of caution, we do anticipate a surge in
IG corporate issuance, on the back of increasing capex and rising M&A deal activity. Given the
picture, we recommend limited short-duration exposure and moving toward the “belly of the
curve” to capture decent coupons with lower price volatility. We see the long end continuing
to be plagued by structural imbalances that show up as widening term premiums.
Opportunistic 
Fixed Income Market-Weight
High yield is competing effectively, with better liquidity, greater transparency and more
favorable credit quality than private credit. In ROW and emerging market debt, yields are
decent, and there may be modest room for spread tightening, as economic growth improves.
Alternative Investments Weight Relative toModel Benchmark  
Real Assets Overweight
We expect higher stock-bond correlations, which place a premium on the diversiﬁcation
beneﬁts of investing in real assets. Nevertheless, with real interest rates positive and services
inﬂation remaining quite sticky, we would need to be selective in adding to this asset class
broadly. We are focused on industrial metals, energy infrastructure and interesting
opportunities aimed at solving the residential housing shortage.
Hedged Strategies Overweight
We added to equity hedged positions early in 2026, noting the pickup in idiosyncratic risk and
rising stock level dispersion. The current environment appears constructive for hedge fund
managers, who are frequently good stock-pickers and can use leverage and risk management
to potentially amplify returns. We prefer very active and fundamental strategies, especially
high-quality, low-beta, low-volatility and absolute return/market neutral strategies.

Glossary
Alpha is the excess return of an investment relative to the return of a benchmark index.
Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI) A ﬁeld of study that seeks to train computers to process large amounts of unstructured information in a manner
similar to human intelligence, capable of performing tasks such as learning and problem solving.
Beta is a measure of the volatility, or systematic risk, of a security or a portfolio in comparison to the market as a whole.
Correlation This is a statistical measure of how two securities move in relation to each other. This measure is often converted into what is
known as correlation coefﬁcient, which ranges between -1 and +1. Perfect positive correlation (a correlation coefﬁcient of +1) implies that as one
security moves, either up or down, the other security will move in lockstep, in the same direction. Alternatively, perfect negative correlation
means that if one security moves in either direction the security that is perfectly negatively correlated will move in the opposite direction. If the
correlation is 0, the movements of the securities are said to have no correlation; they are completely random. A correlation greater than 0.8 is
generally described as strong, whereas a correlation less than 0.5 is generally described as weak. 
Earnings revision breadth is deﬁned as the number of positive analyst revisions minus the number of negative analyst revisions divided by the
total number of revisions. 
Equity risk premium is the excess return that an individual stock or the overall stock market provides over a risk-free rate. The risk-free rate
represents the interest an investor would expect from an absolutely risk-free investment over a speciﬁed period of time.
Term premium is the excess yield that investors require to commit to holding a long-term bond instead of a series of shorter-term bonds.
Price to forward earnings calculates the price-to-earnings ratio that uses projected future earnings.
