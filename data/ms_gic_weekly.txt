INVESTMENT STRATEGY
Last Wednesday, Treasury Secretary Bessent announced at least a
doubling of potential purchases of longer-dated US bonds. While the
immediate but seemingly temporary effect was to stem the sell-off on
the long end of the curve, investors should be asking why the
Treasury chose to do this, and why now. After all, the extraordinary
event comes absent a crisis and amid above average nominal
economic growth, nearly all-time-high equity prices, free-ﬂowing
corporate credit and stellar earnings. Some have surmised that
Bessent is simply testing market reactions or addressing liquidity-
instability perceptions, while others cynically suggest it’s a tease of
stealth QE. While the truth is undoubtedly more nuanced, it should
reawaken investors to several important developments with
implications for risk management. First, long-duration global debt
issuance is growing, fostering yield competition and higher real rates
—a headwind for stock multiples. Second, we are entering a new
regime for policymakers, thus increasing unpredictability and term
premiums. Finally, with dynamics resolved via currency markets,
global rebalancing likely requires a weaker US dollar. Policy can run
but ultimately can’t hide. We suggest benchmark-neutral duration-
positioning focused on three-to-seven-year bonds. Consider
rebalancing ﬁxed income toward strategic benchmarks, neutralizing
tactical tilts, as we expect policy-related volatility to remain above
average. In equities, add to equal-weighted index exposure or active
stock selection. Financials, health care and some defensives ﬁt the
bill, along with select overweights to cloud-dominant hyperscalers.
We still like non-semiconductor-oriented EM and Japan and prefer
intermediate rates to credit, with added diversiﬁcation from hedge
funds, real assets, venture capital and growth equity.
While most investors have spent the summer focused on equity markets and robust
corporate earnings, US Treasuries have been under pressure. Since about mid-June,
the 30-year bond is down more than 6%, with its yield recently piercing 5.3% for a
20-year high. The 10-year yield has punched through 4.7%, to an 18-month high.

Perhaps more importantly, Treasury auctions this month were
weak, with the 30-year bond exhibiting its worst pricing since
2001 and the 10-year note its weakest since 2007.
Admittedly, bond investors have had to discount their share
of surprises this year, but action on the long end of the curve
has not really been explained by inﬂation expectations, which
are well anchored, or near-term economic surprises, which
have actually weakened (see Chart of the Week), but by
material repricing of real rates and term premiums. These
components tend to reﬂect structural changes in long-run
perceptions of the neutral rate of economic growth,
supply/demand dynamics and policy predictability.
It’s obvious why policymakers would watch these
developments. Not only is the 10-year Treasury note the risk-
free benchmark against which other global assets tend to be
priced, but along with that of the 30-year bond, its yield
impacts the all-important mortgage market. Equally critical
are implications for US borrowing costs, which are ballooning
as the Congressional Budget Ofﬁce (CBO) projects a 2026
ﬁscal deﬁcit of close to $2.1 trillion, taking America’s debt
above the $40 trillion milestone. In his initial press
interactions, Federal Reserve Chair Warsh has seemed to
extol letting markets reprice these realities, noting that it’s an
important monetary policy signal. But Secretary Bessent’s
recent actions suggest a more interventionist approach to
rate management. In addition to last week’s surprise
announcement to at least double the pace of long-duration
liquidity-management purchases, on July 31, he announced
highly unusual currency-market intervention aimed at
strengthening the Japanese yen, with the implied objective of
safeguarding Japanese demand for Treasuries. Bessent’s
approach to “liquidity management” will likely be funded by
more front-end issuance, a strategy that has correlated with
tech-stock valuation support. The obvious questions for
investors are, why is Bessent showing more urgency than
Warsh, and why now? These questions seem especially
compelling given that markets appear to be trading without
major stress, credit spreads are near cycle lows, bank lending
is growing at an 8% annual pace and corporate proﬁts have
supported equities. In analyzing Bessent’s actions, there are
several matters for investors to consider.
The ﬁrst issue is that this is not simply about Bessent’s desire
to do liquidity housekeeping on the long end of the curve and
“squeeze out” short sellers. To the contrary, we see the real-
rates backup as a global phenomenon. Per Bloomberg’s
benchmark of global sovereign bonds with maturities of at
least 20 years, the average long-maturity yield is approaching
4.5%. In many cases, this is underpinned by surging debt and
deﬁcits amid spending to retool defense, transition energy
grids and bolster tech infrastructure in a multipolar world.
Then there’s corporate issuance, supercharged by AI-related
capex. Gross investment grade issuance is estimated to reach
a record this year, at more than $2.2 trillion. On the demand
front, many natural buyers—namely, insurance companies
and pension funds—have new choices beyond Treasuries,
with the former increasingly turning to private credit markets
and many of the latter able to own riskier investments given
overfunding. As a result, real rates have accounted for
roughly one-third of the nominal rate move the past two
months, with the 10-year real rate at 2.4%.
Bessent is also keenly aware of rising US interest costs. The
ﬁscal year-2026 cumulative deﬁcit was recently forecast at
$1.9 trillion–$2.1 trillion, 10% above a prior CBO forecast that
already pegged deﬁcits as a share of GDP at close to 6%. At
roughly a 3.5% average effective cost of borrowing, the US
Treasury is projected to pay out more than $1 trillion in
interest, more than two-thirds of the increase in annual deﬁcit
spending. Interest costs already take up 20% of tax revenue
and account for around the same budget share as defense.
When they grow faster than nominal GDP, as now, they
actually add to debt. This is not about hitting a panic button
over sustainability, as the US will not default even if it means
printing money and debasing the greenback. But it is about a
declining degree of ﬁscal-spending freedom. Building policy
uncertainty is evident in the normalizing term premiums that
have accounted for two-thirds of the move in nominal long-
term rates, underscoring declining future policy predictability.
Bottom Line. For several years, the GIC has argued that
structurally faster growth, global releveraging and aging
demographics, against a backdrop of strained debt
sustainability and multipolarity, would likely cause US rates
to normalize toward a “higher for longer” regime. Our thesis
has been that this would actually be healthy, likely
enhancing capital-allocation efﬁciency. This is ﬁnally playing
out, with the ﬁrst genuine competition for longer-duration
capital in nearly two decades. Policy risks are also rising, and
while investors need to be mindful of tactical policymaker
choices, we view non-crisis market intervention and ﬁnancial
engineering attempts as short-lived gimmicks. For long-term
investors, implications remain the same, arguing for well-
diversiﬁed portfolios, as in a higher-for-longer rate
environment stock valuations may plateau while ﬁxed
income is challenged—creating an important role for real
assets and alternatives. Watch long-term yield composition,
noting that an unpredictable policy agenda might only drive
further term-premium normalization. Consider rebalancing
ﬁxed income toward strategic benchmarks, neutralizing
tactical tilts, as we expect policy-related volatility to remain
above average. In equities, add to equal-weighted index
exposure or active stock selection. Financials, health care
and some defensives ﬁt the bill, along with select
overweights to cloud-dominant hyperscalers. We still like
non-semiconductor-oriented EM and Japan and prefer
intermediate rates to credit, with added diversiﬁcation from
hedge funds, real assets, venture capital and growth equity.

Chart of the Week: Long Rates Decoupling From Tactical-Horizon Surprises
For signiﬁcant swaths of time—especially during the past
decade and a half of well-telegraphed monetary policy—
even long-term rates correlated fairly closely with
economic surprises, amid the belief that they represented
the marginal uncertainty in Fed policy direction. But more
recently, long-bond yields, at multiperiod highs, have
strongly decoupled from surprises, suggesting other
forces are at work. We have noted that this is being
fueled by the real and term-premium components of
yields, driving changing perceptions of the neutral rate of
economic growth and policy volatility going forward.
These structural factors could imply that investors should
prepare for a business cycle where rates are higher for
longer while occupying a more normal zone than during the mid-1980s to the Great Financial Crisis.
Fixed Income Insight: Term Premium Breaking Out
Investors were treated to another “surprise” intervention
announcement from Secretary Bessent last week. Bessent
suggested that recent long-duration US Treasury bond
weakness was not driven by fundamentals but impacted
by liquidity technicals and that his solution was at least a
doubling of long-bond buybacks. While his strategies may
partially relieve some bond investors, we have focused on
the longer-term trends suggesting normalizing long-bond
term premiums. This began in earnest with 2021–2022
Fed rate hikes and has continued with policy uncertainty
priced amid shifting geopolitics, surging ﬁscal deﬁcits and
appointment of a new Fed chair. While term premiums of
75 to 85 basis points have been rare in the past 15 years,
they are half the pre-GFC level. At the end of the day, given global capital competition, there is fundamental upward pressure
on rates that the Treasury secretary cannot engineer away.

Tactical Asset Allocation Reasoning
Global Equities Weight Relative to

US Overweight
The productivity-boom thesis catalyzed by the GenAI-capex build-out, along with aggressive ﬁscal
and regulatory stimulus, has become very much consensus. Forecasts are ambitious and embed an
expectation for cyclical broadening that takes "S&P 493" earnings growth from 6%–8% in 2025 to
24%–26% in 2026. In a market that is already expensive, concentrated and complacent, this
suggests the aperture for upside surprise is narrow, and markets are apt to be quite brittle in the
face of exogenous shocks. With midterm-election-related policy already hitting a fever pitch,
potential for these undiscounted events to occur is rising. Thus, while we are bullish (with MS &
Co.'s 2Q2027 S&P 500 Index target at 8,300), we prefer stock-picking to simply owning the cap-
weighted index. Favor ﬁnancials, health care, select industrials and energy.
International Equities
(Developed Markets) Underweight
Fiscal stimulus, a response to the "America First" agenda, has helped to drive recent
outperformance. This is creating rest-of-world (ROW) opportunities that may simultaneously
enjoy monetary, ﬁscal and currency-related stimulus. The outlook is improving in Japan, as
corporate restructuring occurs alongside a reﬂationary surge three decades in the making.
Emerging Markets Overweight
The "new Monroe Doctrine" creates opportunities not only for pro-business political stability in
Latin America, but also for direct investment. For Asia, strategic realignment opportunities
continue to surface, as the US turns away and the US-China trade conﬂict has effectively reached
a one-year truce, fostering opportunities. India remains a recommended secular growth long. The
GIC reduced its overweight after a signiﬁcant rally in semiconductor names, which skewed index
composition.
Global Fixed Income Weight Relative to
Model Benchmark
 
US Investment Grade Underweight
The GIC maintains close-to-neutral views in investment grade. In the ﬁrst half of 2026, solid
growth, supported by ﬁscal stimulus, and inﬂationary pressures supported a higher-for-longer
interest rate environment. Should the Fed maintain a "hold" versus enacting a hike as its next
move, investors could ﬁnd some value in IG. As a point of caution, we do anticipate a surge in IG
corporate issuance, on the back of increasing capex and rising M&A deal activity. Given the
picture, we recommend limited short-duration exposure and moving toward the “belly of the
curve” to capture decent coupons with lower price volatility. We see the long end continuing to
be plagued by structural imbalances that show up as widening term premiums.
Opportunistic Fixed
Income Market-Weight
High yield is competing effectively, with better liquidity, greater transparency and more favorable
credit quality than private credit. In ROW and emerging market debt, yields are decent, and there
may be modest room for spread tightening, as economic growth improves.
Alternative Investments Weight Relative to
Model Benchmark
 
Real Assets Overweight
We expect higher stock-bond correlations, which place a premium on the diversiﬁcation beneﬁts
of investing in real assets. Nevertheless, with real interest rates positive and services inﬂation
remaining quite sticky, we would need to be selective in adding to this asset class broadly. We are
focused on industrial metals, energy infrastructure and interesting opportunities aimed at solving
the residential housing shortage. 
Hedged Strategies Overweight
We added to equity hedged positions early in 2026, noting the pickup in idiosyncratic risk and
rising stock level dispersion. The current environment appears constructive for hedge fund
managers, who are frequently good stock-pickers and can use leverage and risk management to
potentially amplify returns. We prefer very active and fundamental strategies, especially high-
quality, low-beta, low-volatility and absolute return/market neutral strategies.

Glossary
Drawdown refers to the largest cumulative percentage decline in net asset value or the percentage decline from the highest value or net asset
value (peak) to the lowest value net asset value (trough) after the peak.
Earnings revisions breadth is deﬁned as the number of positive analyst revisions minus the number of negative analyst revisions divided by the
total number of revisions.
Equity risk premium is the excess return that an individual stock or the overall stock market provides over a risk-free rate. The risk-free rate
represents the interest an investor would expect from an absolutely risk-free investment over a speciﬁed period of time.
Illiquidity premium is the extra yield investors expect to earn for giving up control to liquidate their capital for a certain period of time.
M2 is a measure of the money supply that includes all elements of M1 as well as "near money." M1 includes cash and checking deposits, while
near money refers to savings deposits, money market securities, mutual funds and other time deposits.
Nominal Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is the GDP of the country measured at current market prices and not adjusted for inﬂation or
deﬂation.
Option-adjusted spread (OAS) is a measurement of the spread of a ﬁxed income security rate and the risk-free rate of return, which is adjusted
to take into account an embedded option.
Price to forward earnings calculates the price-to-earnings ratio that uses projected future earnings.
R-star is the real short-term interest rate expected to prevail when an economy is at full strength and inﬂation is stable.
Risk premium is the return in excess of the risk-free rate of return an investment is expected to yield.
Term premium is the excess yield that investors require to commit to holding a long-term bond instead of a series of shorter-term bonds.
Volatility This is a statistical measure of the dispersion of returns for a given security or market index. Volatility can either be measured by using
the standard deviation or variance between returns from that same security or market index. Commonly, the higher the volatility, the riskier the
security.

MLPs
Master Limited Partnerships (MLPs) are limited partnerships or limited liability companies that are taxed as partnerships and whose interests
(limited partnership units or limited liability company units) are traded on securities exchanges like shares of common stock. Currently, most
MLPs operate in the energy, natural resources or real estate sectors. Investments in MLP interests are subject to the risks generally applicable
to companies in the energy and natural resources sectors, including commodity pricing risk, supply and demand risk, depletion risk and
exploration risk. Individual MLPs are publicly traded partnerships that have unique risks related to their structure. These include, but are not
limited to, their reliance on the capital markets to fund growth, adverse ruling on the current tax treatment of distributions (typically mostly
tax deferred), and commodity volume risk. The potential tax beneﬁts from investing in MLPs depend on their being treated as partnerships for
federal income tax purposes and, if the MLP is deemed to be a corporation, then its income would be subject to federal taxation at the entity
level, reducing the amount of cash available for distribution to the fund which could result in a reduction of the fund’s value.
MLPs carry interest rate risk and may underperform in a rising interest rate environment. MLP funds accrue deferred income taxes for future tax
liabilities associated with the portion of MLP distributions considered to be a tax-deferred return of capital and for any net operating gains as
well as capital appreciation of its investments; this deferred tax liability is reﬂected in the daily NAV; and, as a result, the MLP fund’s after-tax
performance could differ signiﬁcantly from the underlying assets even if the pre-tax performance is closely tracked.

Ultrashort bond funds Ultra-short bond funds are mutual funds and exchange-traded funds that generally invest in ﬁxed income securities with

Duration, the most commonly used measure of bond risk, quantiﬁes the effect of changes in interest rates on the price of a bond or bond
portfolio. The longer the duration, the more sensitive the bond or portfolio would be to changes in interest rates. Generally, if interest rates
rise, bond prices fall and vice versa. Longer-term bonds carry a longer or higher duration than shorter-term bonds; as such, they would be
affected by changing interest rates for a greater period of time if interest rates were to increase. Consequently, the price of a long-term bond
would drop signiﬁcantly as compared to the price of a short-term bond.
