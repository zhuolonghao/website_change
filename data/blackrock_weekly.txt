CAPITAL AT RISK. MARKETING MATERIAL.
Opening frame: What’s driving markets? Market take
Camera frame
Title slide: AI: looking beyond the buildout
Amid recent tech stock swings, we’re reconciling our longer-term AI conviction – which is a strategic overweight, as evidenced in our latest update to our capital market assumptions – with nearer-term caution – our tactical 6–12 month horizon view is neutral.
1: The price is right?
The picture for U.S. stocks depends on how you measure valuations. One measure suggests stocks are almost as expensive as they were during the dot-com bubble.
Another paints a less extreme picture. But that's because investors are counting on strong earnings growth.
Whether AI can deliver that growth is still an open question. For one, cheaper computing could squeeze profits for AI suppliers. Meanwhile, bottlenecks could slow how quickly businesses adopt AI. But while both could weigh on the AI theme in the near term, they don’t necessarily undermine the longer-term opportunity.
2: The longer-term opportunity
Now, there’s undoubtedly plenty of uncertainty over how AI will play out. And any one set of long-run capital market assumptions is conditional: it reflects one assumed path for the economy. That’s why BlackRock’s capital market assumptions are built around multiple scenarios with materially different macro outcomes.
Companies could build more AI capacity than needed, and profit growth could slow before businesses see the benefits. Or, on the upside, a productivity boom could come earlier and be even more significant than current estimates.
Our longer-term conviction doesn't depend on today's AI winners staying on top. Instead, it's about what happens as AI gets better and more businesses start using it. That could boost productivity, profit margins and earnings over time. We see particular opportunities in U.S. health care, technology and financial companies.
3: Higher rates, stronger bonds
Our conviction in AI's longer-term earnings potential underpins our strategic overweight in U.S. stocks. That, in turn, is a key reason we favor developed-market stocks more broadly. But realizing AI's potential will take huge investment.
Companies building AI are competing with governments for funding, adding pressure on interest rates.
That's led us to raise our longer-term rate expectations. Yet materially higher market yields have also made bonds more attractive, now. For the first time since 2020, we're taking a more positive view on interest rate risk, especially favoring inflation-linked bonds.
Outro: Here’s our Market take
Short-term uncertainty hasn't shaken our long-term conviction in AI.
We still favor U.S. stocks over the longer term. Higher yields are giving us more reason to favor bonds, especially those that protect against inflation.
Closing frame: Read details: blackrock.com/weekly-commentary
Looking long-term
Our longer-term conviction on the AI transformation underpins our strategic overweight in U.S. equities, even as near-term risks warrant tactical caution.
Market backdrop
The S&P 500 recovered most of its post-election losses, helped by AI stocks. The rally suggests AI earnings optimism still outweighs higher rates, for now.
Week ahead
We eye fresh inflation data in Japan and across the euro zone for clues on how higher energy costs are impacting price pressures.
Recent swings in tech stocks reflect growing uncertainty over how the AI buildout will evolve and what that means for earnings. We downgraded U.S. stocks tactically last month, but our longer-term conviction rests on AI’s improving capabilities and adoption over time – not on today’s earnings growth or winners staying on top.
Our latest capital market assumptions (for professional investors only) reinforce our strategic overweight in U.S. equities over a horizon of five years or longer.

A tale of two valuations
S&P 500 cyclically adjusted and 12-month forward P/E ratios, 2000–26

Notes: CAPE uses 10-year inflation-adjusted earnings; forward P/E uses 12-month earnings estimates. Shading marks the dot-com bust (2000–02), global financial crisis (2007–09) and COVID-19 (2020).
The valuation picture for U.S. equities depends on the measure used. The Shiller cyclically adjusted price-to-earnings ratio (CAPE) suggests valuations are near dot-com bubble highs. See the chart. The S&P 500’s 12-month forward P/E looks less stretched by historical standards, but only because investors expect strong earnings growth. Whether those expectations are justified is an open question. The key uncertainty is how the AI buildout evolves relative to demand and adoption. Falling computing costs could squeeze suppliers’ profits, while bottlenecks could delay AI adoption. Both could cause near-term pressure, but neither necessarily undermines the longer-term opportunity, in our view.
Ample uncertainty still hangs over the longer-term picture. That’s a key feature of the polyfurcated environment we’ve long described, where multiple scenarios are plausible. Overcapacity is a risk, and builders' earnings could slow before adopters see meaningful gains. Our strategic conviction doesn't assume that current winners will remain winners. Instead, our starting-point scenario explicitly sees valuations declining without assuming a permanent growth breakout. Yet AI’s improving capabilities could drive broader adoption, boosting productivity, profit margins and earnings over time. We see particular opportunities in U.S. health care, IT, tech, and financials, where stronger longer-term earnings prospects help underpin our strategic overweight in U.S. equities, a key driver of our broader developed-market equity overweight.
A longer runway
The longer-term AI opportunity, however, requires substantial investment to make it a reality. Companies financing the AI buildout are competing with governments borrowing heavily from the same pool of savings, intensifying competition for capital. That backdrop has led us to revise up our interest rate expectations in our CMAs. We expect central bank policy rates to stay elevated for longer, reflecting a higher expected neutral interest rate. Yet stronger growth and earnings can coexist with higher borrowing costs, and a higher-rate environment is increasingly reflected in market prices.
That repricing has improved bonds’ strategic appeal, with higher starting yields offering better compensation for longer-term interest rate risk. For the first time since 2020, we are taking a more constructive view on duration risk. We have moved to neutral on developed-market government bonds and strengthened our overweight in inflation-linked bonds, which offer attractive yields and protection against inflation risks tied to supply constraints. Yet government bonds are no longer the all-weather diversifiers they once were. Persistent inflation pressures limit central banks’ ability to cut rates and drive bond yields lower, reducing the scope for bond prices to rise. That’s also why we favor building portfolios granularly around specific exposures and convictions rather than asset class labels, reflecting a broader industry shift toward a total portfolio approach.
Our bottom line
We maintain our strategic overweight in U.S. equities, backed by our conviction in AI’s longer-term earnings potential. Higher yields also strengthen the case for bonds, particularly inflation-linked bonds.
Market backdrop
U.S. stocks recovered nearly all their post-midterm election losses last week as an AI-led rebound gave the S&P 500 its best week since early August. Chip stocks led the gains, with the Philadelphia Semiconductor index surging 8%. The rebound suggests AI earnings optimism is outweighing pressure from higher interest rates, for now. Two-year U.S. Treasury yields jumped 33 basis points to 4.86% after hawkish comments from Fed officials and a hotter-than-expected inflation report.
We’re watching European and Japanese inflation data for signs that higher energy costs are fueling broader price pressures, potentially prompting further interest rate hikes. European consumer confidence will also offer clues on how households are coping with higher costs and what that means for the region’s economic outlook.

Intersecting mega forces
Since we launched our mega forces framework it has become clearer how their intersection shapes almost all our investment views and opens up alpha opportunities. They cut across asset class labels, spurring a rethink of portfolio construction. Investors need to be deliberate about the economic or thematic exposures they own, the vehicles they use to implement them and their investment horizons.

Six to 12-month tactical views on selected assets vs. broad global asset classes by level of conviction, November 2026
We have lengthened our tactical investment horizon back to six to 12 months. The table below reflects this and, importantly, leaves aside the opportunity for alpha, or the potential to generate above-benchmark returns – especially at a time of heightened volatility.

Europe ex UK
We are neutral. We would need to see more business-friendly policy and deeper capital markets for Europe to outperform and to justify a broad overweight. We stay selective, favoring financials, infrastructure, industrials, materials and healthcare.
Germany
We are neutral. Higher spending on defense and infrastructure support the corporate sector. Valuations are not stretched but neither compelling and expected earnings growth lags other countries. Light positioning means potential easing in geopolitical tensions and AI lifting industrials-driven earnings could create opportunities.
France
We are neutral. Strong expected earnings growth and global revenue exposure support French corporates. Valuations are less compelling, albeit not stretched versus euro area peers. Persistent political uncertainty leaves the overall risk reward balanced.
Italy
We are neutral. Earnings growth and momentum are strong, boosted by the significant exposure to financials, utilities and energy. Valuations are still at discount vs. peers, but not as much as in previous years. Political risk is currently low but likely to pick up ahead of 2027 elections.
Spain
We are overweight. Valuations remain attractive vs. peers even if relative earnings momentum has slowed. Strong domestic demand growth and exposure to fast-growing areas like Latin America support Spanish stocks. Financials, utilities and infrastructure-linked stocks would benefit from Europe’s push towards autonomy.
Netherlands
We are neutral. Earnings revisions in the IT sector, a large sector in the Dutch stock market is offset by other sectors seeing less favorable valuations and a weaker earnings outlook than European peers.
Switzerland
We are neutral. Valuations have improved, from stretched levels, but the earnings outlook is weaker than other European markets. If global risk appetite stays strong, the index’s tilt to stable, less volatile sectors may weigh on performance .

German bunds
We are neutral Bunds. Current ECB rate pricing looks more fairly valued given the balance of risks. Fiscal stimulus and increased bond issuance exert upward pressure on yields, alongside inflation risks amid lingering geopolitical tensions.
French OATs
We are neutral. Elevated political uncertainty, high budget deficits and slow structural reforms could stoke volatility, but these risks already seem priced into OATs and we don’t expect a material worsening from here.
Italian BTPs
We are neutral. The spread over German bunds looks tight given Italy’s large budget deficits and growing public debt. Domestic factors remain supportive, with growth holding up relative to the rest of the euro area and local demand for BTPs solid at current yield levels. Domestic disapproval would likely prevent defense spending from reaching fiscally unstable levels. Political uncertainty is likely to increase ahead of next year’s elections.
UK gilts
We are neutral. We expect periods of elevated volatility given political uncertainty, longer-term bonds making up a larger market share, and buyers becoming more price-sensitive.
Swiss government bonds
We are neutral. The SNB seems comfortable with its medium-term inflation outlook and its current policy stance. Market pricing is broadly in line with SNB messaging.
European inflation-protected securities
We are neutral. We see higher medium-term inflation, but inflation expectations are firmly anchored. Cooling inflation and uncertain growth may matter more near term.
European investment grade
European investment grade is supported by healthy corporate sector balance sheets, contained default rates and the persistently strong demand for durable income from European households. We prefer European investment grade over the U.S.
European high yield
We are overweight. While spreads are low, the income potential remains attractive. Defaults are contained and high yield is of higher quality and less sensitive to interest rate swings compared with the U.S.
