CAPITAL AT RISK. MARKETING MATERIAL.
Opening frame: What’s driving markets? Market take
Camera frame
Title slide: The Middle East energy shock’s next phase
The global economy proved resilient to the Middle East energy shock. Now, buffers that helped soften the initial hit to economic growth like strategic reserves and rerouted oil shipments are weakening, and supply constraints are moving downstream.
1: A more nuanced story
Brent crude oil jumped to a five-month high last week. But oil prices are only part of the evolving Middle East conflict story. Early on, the focus was on crude supply. Now, the pressure has moved downstream to refining and shipping bottlenecks. That’s pushed up the delivered cost of fuel and helped refinery and tanker stocks far outpace major oil and gas producers. After those sharp gains, what matters now is which bottlenecks persist, even if the conflict ends.
2: Uneven effects
The global economy has weathered the energy shock better than expected, thanks to AI investment and government spending, but not everyone is feeling it equally. Europe is more vulnerable, particularly with gas reserves at historically low levels heading into winter. Meanwhile, the U.S. has so far had a much bigger cushion as a net energy exporter.
That’s accelerating the push for more resilient energy systems. Importers are diversifying their energy supplies, while exporters are investing in new routes around vulnerable chokepoints. We think that shift could outlast the conflict, creating investment opportunities but also requiring huge amounts of capital and putting upward pressure on government bond yields.
3: Selectivity is key
So, where does that leave us as investors? The focus is on refining and energy infrastructure, or where bottlenecks could persist. We’re also selective across regions, with Europe more exposed to higher energy costs and the U.S. less exposed to the worst outcomes.
In bonds, higher yields have made U.S. Treasuries more attractive. We prefer shorter-term bonds for their income, as the investment needed to strengthen energy security could put upward pressure on longer-term yields.
Outro: Here’s our Market take
We think some of today’s energy constraints could outlast the Middle East conflict. That keeps us selective, favoring opportunities where energy constraints and margins look durable.

Scarcity pays downstream
Selected oil industry sub-sector equity performance, 2026

Brent crude oil surged back to around $117 a barrel last week, its highest since April. But oil prices tell only part of the story of how the energy shock has evolved. The initial disruption centered on crude supply, but rerouted shipments, inventories and strategic reserves helped absorb much of the shock. Now scarcity has shifted downstream. Even as crude supplies recover, refining and shipping bottlenecks have pushed the delivered cost of fuel from the Middle East to Asia to more than twice prewar levels. This helps explain why shares of refiners have jumped some 160% this year, while shares of tanker operators have climbed 120%. See the chart. Both have far outpaced the 40% gain in major oil and gas producers, even as energy has become the top-performing sector globally. But after such sharp gains, how much further these stocks can run depends on which bottlenecks persist, even if the conflict ends.
Global growth has held up thanks to strong AI investment and government spending. Much of the initial inflation shock is already reflected in headline inflation, but higher fuel costs are starting to feed into some core inflation categories. As we’ve previously noted, the energy shock has uneven effects: we estimate it’s reduced GDP by about 0.5% in Europe, compared with just 0.1% in the U.S. Those differences could become even more pronounced. Europe looks particularly vulnerable, with natural gas storage at around 75% – its lowest seasonal level since recordkeeping began in 2011 – while disrupted supplies of liquefied natural gas complicate efforts to rebuild reserves. A colder winter could push European energy prices, already up 170% this year, higher. The U.S. is better insulated as a net energy exporter, while strong tech exports have cushioned Asia from higher import costs. The shock’s uneven impacts are accelerating efforts to bolster energy resilience and security.
Selectivity is key
That is creating new investment opportunities – but at a cost. The geopolitical fragmentation mega force we’ve long described reinforces this shift, raising the value of infrastructure that protects against future shocks. Energy importers are diversifying both traditional fuel supplies and electrification, underscoring the energy transition and resilience mega force. Exporters, including those in the Middle East, are investing in alternative routes to bypass vulnerable chokepoints. We see this as a structural shift that could outlast the conflict. Yet building these contingencies requires substantial investment, which could intensify competition for capital and put further upward pressure on sovereign bond yields.
We favor opportunities where energy constraints and profit margins look durable and valuations remain attractive. For now, existing energy equity exposures could deliver strong earnings as oil prices and refining margins stay elevated. But after this year’s oil rally, we also see opportunities in select U.S. and European utilities hit by rising bond yields. In bonds, higher yields support our recent upgrade to U.S. Treasuries. We prefer short-term bonds for income, as growing energy security investment could increase competition for capital and push longer-term yields higher.
Our bottom line
The Mideast energy shock is exposing longer-term constraints that could outlast the conflict. We favor selective exposure to energy infrastructure and short-term income over broad market bets.
Market backdrop
Tech stocks swung sharply last week as investors stepped up scrutiny of the AI trade, pushing the Nasdaq down roughly 1% despite strong earnings from some of the sector’s biggest names. The S&P 500 fell 0.4%, with a rotation into defensive sectors helping limit losses. U.S. 10-year Treasury yields slipped below 5% as investors sought refuge from the tech selloff. Brent crude oil touched a six-month high of $117 a barrel on concerns about further disruption to Middle East energy flows.
The Federal Reserve’s interest rate decision takes center stage this week after a hotter-than-expected September CPI report showed price pressures broadening. Even as we expect another hike by year-end, we don’t view recent tightening as the start of a sustained hiking cycle. Markets have partially scaled back expectations for further hikes since the September meeting, moving closer to our view.

Intersecting mega forces
Since we launched our mega forces framework it has become clearer how their intersection shapes almost all our investment views and opens up alpha opportunities. They cut across asset class labels, spurring a rethink of portfolio construction. Investors need to be deliberate about the economic or thematic exposures they own, the vehicles they use to implement them and their investment horizons.

European inflation-protected securities

European investment grade
European investment grade is supported by healthy corporate sector balance sheets, contained default rates and the persistently strong demand for durable income from European households. We prefer European investment grade over the U.S.
